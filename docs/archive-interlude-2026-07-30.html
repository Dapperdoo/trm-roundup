THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football. Just thirteen managers. God help us.

There are no fixtures today. The World Cup Final is still ahead of us, and between now and then we have nothing — no goals, no blanks, no last-minute heartbreak to report. Just a quiet afternoon, a blinking cursor, and thirteen grown adults who have been staring at a spreadsheet for weeks pretending it's a personality. So. We turn the spotlight on the managers themselves. Pull up a chair. This is going to be warm, affectionate, and almost entirely their fault.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and someone has to acknowledge it before he does. Shatner's Bassoon leads the league by a distance that is, frankly, embarrassing for everyone else. Joe will tell you this is the result of careful research and shrewd transfer instincts. What it actually is, is a man who spends his evenings wrestling AI tools into doing basic tasks — a doomed, faintly heroic crusade that never quite delivers — and yet somehow, in the gaps between the software lying to his face, he's assembled the best squad in the building. His flat is a cheerful tip. There's probably a half-finished Guinness somewhere near the laptop. The league is losing to a man whose filing system is 'the floor'. Tristan, his main rival at third, is throwing his hands up so hard he's pulled something.

Tristan, for his part, runs his operation off Jeremy's legendary spreadsheet — every number gleaned by hand, painstakingly, from the FIFA website, one tab at a time, like a man transcribing the Rosetta Stone with a biro. He does not suffer fools, which is unfortunate because this league is essentially a fool convention. Jeremy built that spreadsheet from scratch, by the way — coded the whole thing, architected it beautifully — and then, in a move of supreme artistic restraint, decided he would pick his own team using nothing but instinct, taste and thirty years of watching football. No algorithm. No system. Just the organic human brain, which Jeremy's happens to be considerably better than most. He watches Lloydy's AI circus with the quiet, withering expression of a master craftsman watching someone use a bandsaw to butter toast.

Speaking of which. Chris. Lloydy. The mad scientist at ninth. The algorithm. We've all heard about the algorithm. In the last transfer window, it fired five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup — men who are four years older, some of whom have since retired, some of whom are probably coaching youth teams in the suburbs — before finally, mercifully, landing on a single valid player who actually exists in this tournament and scored eight points. Eight! The algorithm celebrated. Lloydy celebrated. Jake, at twelfth, did not celebrate. Jake has been vindicated so completely that he's started saying 'I told you so' in his sleep. He shouts it. His neighbours have complained.

Jake. Snacob's Ladder. A renewable-energy project manager who has never, in recorded history, been injured doing anything remotely dangerous. He once hurt himself on the way to the dentist. On the way. Not at the dentist — though he loves the dentist, genuinely adores it, will talk about his dental hygienist with a warmth most people reserve for close family — but en route, presumably by tripping over his own enthusiasm. He spent a fortune on Harry Kane and filled the rest of the squad with players so obscure they make up their own Wikipedia pages. He still shouts 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' into rooms apropos of nothing, just to remind himself who he is. He also dances. With drinks. There are people in this league who are still damp.

Joe S, meanwhile, is at sixth with 338 points and the philosophical serenity of a man who has spent more of his football career on a treatment table than on a pitch and has simply made his peace with it. Supremely gifted. Universally loved. Absolutely baffling to ask about his phone. Joe S once had a career that could have been legendary, and it was — in the physio room, where he was basically a resident. The man's injury record reads like a medical textbook. He and Jake should start a support group. They could call it 'Things That Happened To Our Bodies For No Reason At All'.

Sam is fourth — Look at his face, just LOOK at it — and that face is currently doing something between delight and mild panic, which is Sam's factory setting. Stage and screen performer, cricket devotee, Shakespeare enthusiast, beer aficionado, and a man who once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar. Moved in. No bathroom. Stood in the kitchen, looked at the bar, made a decision he has never fully explained. His brother Wigs is right behind him at tenth, which means the family WhatsApp is either very competitive or very supportive, and given that Wigs is a counsellor, probably both at the same time and with excellent boundaries.

Tom, at eighth, is somewhere. We're not entirely sure where. There was a report — barely coherent, very happy — suggesting he was last seen in a field. Nick, his main rival, is up at second with 370 points and a playing style on the actual football pitch that can only be described as 'legally questionable'. Very tall, very loud, shoots like a man trying to clear a barn roof with a nine iron, tackles like he's settling personal scores. Nick doesn't just foul people; he files them. Dan, at seventh, is living off-grid and winning anyway, which is the most Dan thing imaginable. His ally Malik is managing from Iceland at the bottom of the table, signing anyone who has ever glanced at a Manchester United or Portugal shirt — both of whom, it should be noted, are now eliminated from this tournament, which means Malik's transfer philosophy has been geographically and historically retired in one go. Dave, at eleventh, is somewhere between a pun and a poem, dreaming of the day The Culture Show calls and gives him his 'Trossy's Wafflecock' segment — a six-minute meditation on absolutely nothing, delivered with complete sincerity. The cymbals are polished. The pitch is ready. They haven't called.

So that's the room. Thirteen managers, one trophy left to play for, and a final still to come. When the football returns, so does The Morning After — and with it, the points, the pain, and the inevitable moment where someone's squad does something completely inexplicable. Rest up. It's nearly time.

The World Cup Final is on the horizon — one match, one champion, and one last chance for the standings to do something dramatic. The Morning After will be back when the football is. Try not to injure yourselves getting there. Jake, that means you specifically.
