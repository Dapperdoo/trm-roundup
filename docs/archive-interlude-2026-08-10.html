THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football. Just thirteen managers. God help us.

Right. There are no fixtures today. The World Cup has paused for breath, the players are resting their legs, and the tournament has reached the business end — one final match to come, and that's your lot. Which means we have nothing to report. No goals, no points, no drama. So instead, we're going to do something arguably more entertaining: we're going to talk about you lot. Thirteen managers. One spotlight. No escape.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a league lead that is starting to look, frankly, embarrassing. Four hundred and sixty-one. The man spends a significant portion of his waking life arguing with AI tools that refuse to do what they promise — a doomed, Sisyphean crusade conducted largely in his pants with a Guinness — and yet here he is, top of the table. The rest of us are using spreadsheets, instinct, and in at least one case a time machine, and Joe A is winning by ninety-one points. He didn't even dominate the transfer window. He just made a few sensible calls and let everyone else implode around him. Tristan, his great rival, sits third on 364, and you can almost hear the hands going up in disgust from here. Almost, Tristan. Almost.

Now, Nick is second, which is extraordinary given that Nick approaches both football and life like a man who has been handed a nine-iron on the first tee and told the hole is somewhere over that hill. On the pitch he is a reckless, clumping menace — tackles that aren't so much mistimed as pre-planned disasters, launched with the confidence of someone who has never once considered the consequences. His shooting sails high, wide, and into the car park. And yet: 370 points, second place. Loud, enormous, and inexplicably effective. His rival Tom is eighth on 321, which is the kind of gap that would sting — if Tom were currently in a position to feel it, which is not guaranteed.

Speaking of which. Sam is fourth on 361, which is a genuinely strong position for a man whose decision-making process includes signing a year-long tenancy on a house because it had a bar, only to discover on moving day that it had no bathroom. Not a small bathroom. No bathroom. The bar was there. The toilet was not. Sam looked at those empty barrels and made a calculation that tells you everything you need to know about his priorities. He's also a Shakespeare man, a cricket man, and a stage performer — all of which makes him almost insufferably cultured, except that his brother Wigs is tenth on 319 and the sibling scoreboard is not looking great for the counsellor. Wigs is gentle, gregarious, and presumably offering the rest of the table some very affordable therapy rates. He'll need them.

Jeremy is fifth on 360, which is exactly where you'd expect the professor to be — measured, composed, quietly excellent. He built the spreadsheet this entire league runs on. He could, if he wanted, build an algorithm to pick his team. He has chosen not to. He picks on instinct, taste, and experience, like a man selecting wine by smell rather than by app. He watches Chris's algorithmic adventures with the expression of a senior surgeon watching a first-year medical student attempt open-heart surgery with a spoon. Chris, bless him, is ninth on 320 — one point ahead of Wigs — and his much-discussed AI transfer system recently fired five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup before landing one valid pick. Five bids. For players from four years ago. The algorithm wasn't scouting; it was holding a séance. Chris built a transfer engine and got a time machine.

Which brings us to Jake. Jake is twelfth on 292, and he has never been more vindicated in his life, and he absolutely cannot stop telling everyone about it. 'Snacob's Ladder!' he announces, to no one in particular, at regular intervals throughout the day. He overspent heroically on Harry Kane and filled the remaining slots with players of such obscurity they may not technically exist, and yet he burns with the righteous fury of a man who has been proven correct about AI. He also, it should be noted, recently injured himself on the way to the dentist — not at the dentist, not leaving the dentist, but en route, which is a level of commitment to physical misfortune that deserves its own medical literature. The dentist, incidentally, remains Jake's favourite place on earth. He loves it there. He would move in if they'd let him. He also still hasn't forgiven Jeremy for the transfer correction from a couple of days ago — an honest administrative fix that every other manager waved through without a murmur. Jake has filed it away next to the AI grievance and the Kane receipt, where it will live forever.

Joe S is sixth on 338, and if you want to understand Joe Sheerin you need to understand that he is a genuinely brilliant footballer who spent roughly half his career horizontal in a physio room while the game carried on without him. Supremely gifted. Chronically unavailable. The treatment table knows his face better than most defenders ever did. He is also utterly baffled by modern technology, which means he and Jake share a suspicion of AI but for completely different reasons — Jake is paranoid, Joe S just can't find the login page. Dan is seventh on 329, living off-grid and thriving, which is either admirable or suspicious. His ally Malik is thirteenth — last, on 280 — managing remotely from Iceland with a transfer policy that can be summarised as: has he played for Manchester United? Has he played for Portugal? Grand, he'll do. Portugal, of course, have been eliminated. Malik is taking this with the quiet dignity of a man who knew the risks and signed them anyway.

And then there's Dave. Eleventh on 300. Poet. Lecturer. Cymbal enthusiast — though only on special occasions, which raises more questions than it answers. Dave has spent years nurturing a dream of fronting a segment on The Culture Show called Trossy's Wafflecock, in which he would hold forth on cultural nothingness with complete sincerity and absolute conviction. It has not happened yet. The Culture Show has not called. But Dave remains ready, and honestly, in a World Cup with one match left and thirteen managers staring into the void, Trossy's Wafflecock feels more necessary than ever.

One match remains. The Final. Everything to play for, or nothing, depending on which side of the table you're sitting on. The points are banked, the eliminated nations are gone — Algeria, Argentina, Brazil, France, Portugal, the USA, and a dozen others, all done — and whatever happens next happens once, and then it's over. The Morning After will be back with the full reckoning. Try not to injure yourselves between now and then. Jake, that means you especially.
