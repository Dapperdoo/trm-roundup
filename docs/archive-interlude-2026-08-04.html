THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. Thirteen suspects. No alibi.

Good morning. There are no fixtures today — the tournament pauses for breath before the final, and so, with no goals to report and no points to argue over, we turn the spotlight where it has always belonged: on the thirteen people who have been making catastrophically confident decisions for the past several weeks. Pull up a chair. This is the bit where we talk about you.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a one-man lead that looks more comfortable than it actually is. Joe's great gift, as far as anyone can tell, is that he spends enormous amounts of his free time trying to get AI tools to do what they promise — a project that has never once fully worked, and yet he persists, like a man repeatedly feeding coins into a broken vending machine and expecting a different crisp. The irony, of course, is that the league's actual AI enthusiast — more on him shortly — has been an unmitigated disaster, while Joe, who just wanted the software to write a decent email, is somehow top of the table. Hot on his heels: Tristan, the big Russian Londoner who runs his entire operation off a spreadsheet, inputs every number by hand from the FIFA website, and still finds time to throw his hands up in absolute disgust at everything. Tristan doesn't suffer fools. He does, however, apparently suffer sweeties, which is why there are always wrappers on the desk next to the laptop. He's three points behind Joe A and absolutely furious about it.

Then there's Nick, second in the league, six foot something of reckless menace. On the football pitch Nick doesn't so much tackle as launch himself at the general vicinity of a person and hope for the best — badly timed, frequently dangerous, occasionally deliberate, always spectacular. His shooting is the stuff of legend: a wild, full-body golf swing that sends the ball somewhere between the top corner and low Earth orbit. He's never scored a tidy goal in his life and he's never going to start. Somehow, though, his fantasy team is in second place, which suggests that picking players is, mercifully, a non-contact sport. His main rival is Tom, who is eighth, and who — let's be honest — may not know that yet, depending on what time zone he's currently in and whether the signal reaches wherever he is. Tom plays psy-trance. Only psy-trance. Any eyewitness report from the field comes back incoherent but very, very happy. We wish him well.

Speaking of the walking wounded: Joe S. Back of the Van United. Sixth place. A man whose playing career was so comprehensively derailed by injuries that he spent roughly half of it horizontal on a treatment table, which is a shame, because when he was upright he was genuinely brilliant. Joe S is universally loved, which is the other thing about him — you can't even be properly annoyed. He's just there, charming, baffled by his phone, somehow still standing. Meanwhile Jake — twelfth, Snacob's Ladder, yes he's shouting it, he's always shouting it — has an injury record that rivals Joe S's in sheer creative misfortune. Jake doesn't injure himself playing sport. He injures himself on the way to the dentist. Which, and this cannot be stressed enough, he was going to voluntarily and with great enthusiasm, because Jake loves the dentist. Loves it. He will tell you about his last appointment at length. He is currently nursing a grudge against Jeremy that is roughly the same size and shape as a structural wall, over a transfer correction that every other manager accepted and moved on from approximately forty-eight hours ago. Jake has not moved on. Jake will not move on. 'Snacob's Ladder!' he shouts, into the middle distance, at no one.

Which brings us to Jeremy — fifth, Von Neumann Trombone, the professor. Jeremy built the spreadsheet that underpins this entire league from scratch. He is a brilliant programmer. He picks his team on instinct, taste and experience, because he is also the kind of man who could build a clock but prefers to read the sun. He watches Chris's algorithmic adventures with the quiet, withering expression of a master craftsman watching someone try to assemble furniture with a leaf blower. Chris — ninth, Lloyd's Food and Wine, never sits still for more than forty seconds — built an AI transfer system that, in its most recent outing, submitted five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup before eventually, accidentally, landing on someone current. That someone scored 8 points. The algorithm is vindicated, says Chris. The algorithm tried to sign players who have since retired, says everyone else. Several of those players are now, thanks to recent eliminations, in roughly the same bracket as the 2022 squad anyway — which is either poetic justice or a sign that the bot was simply ahead of its time. Jake, for his part, does not find this funny. Jake finds it deeply suspicious and has theories.

Down in the middle of the table, doing interesting things quietly: Dan, seventh, Denton Burn, off-grid musician, historically excellent fantasy manager, witty in a way that catches you off guard. His main ally is Malik — thirteenth, Propaganda Parade, managing from Iceland, signing anyone who has ever pulled on a Manchester United or Portugal shirt. Portugal, of course, are eliminated. This is a fact Malik is invited to sit with. Dan and Malik operate as a kind of two-man mutual appreciation society at opposite ends of the geographical and points spectrum, which is very sweet and has not, so far, translated into a title challenge for either of them.

Meanwhile, the brothers O'Shea. Sam — fourth, Look at his Face, Just Look at his FACE — is a stage and screen performer who loves Shakespeare, beer, dancing, and cricket, roughly in that order depending on the day. He is witty, scatty, and the sort of person who once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar, only to discover on moving day that the property contained no bathroom whatsoever. He eyed the empty barrels. He considered his options. We don't ask further questions. His brother Wigs — tenth, 50 Shades of O'Shea — is a counsellor, which means he has spent a professional lifetime listening patiently to people, and is therefore uniquely equipped to sit across from Sam and nod slowly. They both love cricket. They are both in this league. Neither of them is going to win it, but they'll have excellent opinions about why.

And finally, Dave — eleventh, Trossy's Giants, Trossy Ginge — lecturer, poet, lover of wordplay, city-break tourist, occasional cymbalist. Dave still dreams of his segment on The Culture Show: 'Trossy's Wafflecock', in which he would ramble about cultural nothingness with the heartfelt conviction of a man who has genuinely convinced himself that highbrow intellectual back-and-forth means something. He would be magnificent. He would be entirely unwatchable. He would be both of those things at the same time, which is, in fairness, the best kind of television. Dave is eleven points off the top half of the table and has a pun ready for every occasion. We salute him.

That's your thirteen. No football today, but the final is coming — one last fixture, one last chance to make or break a season. The standings are tighter than they look, the grudges are very much alive, and somewhere out there Jake is already on his way to the dentist. We'll be back when the football is.
