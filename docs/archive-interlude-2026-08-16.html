THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No ball, all receipts — the managers take the stage

There are no fixtures today. The World Cup has reached its final and the dust is settling across the bracket. Twenty-five nations have been eliminated, the last two are still standing, and this league has absolutely nothing to report on the football front. So we're doing something different. Pull up a chair. The Morning After is turning the spotlight away from the pitch and directly onto the thirteen people who have been making our lives interesting all tournament. Consider this a roast, a group therapy session, and a best-man speech rolled into one. Nobody is safe. Everyone is loved. Let's go.

We'll start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a lead that looks comfortable until you actually look at it. He built that cushion with some decent early signings and has been coasting on it ever since, spending his spare time in what can only be described as a one-man war against artificial intelligence — not Lloydy's kind, but the consumer kind, the kind that promises to summarise your emails and instead hallucinates an entirely different inbox. Joe A's relationship with AI tools is that of a man who keeps feeding coins into a broken vending machine, absolutely certain that this time the Twix will drop. It never drops, Joe. It never drops. Meanwhile Tristan, ninety-seven points behind and closing, is doing his data-gathering the old-fashioned way: manually, by hand, from the FIFA website, one cell at a time, like a man transcribing the Bible with a biro. He throws his hands up in disgust at roughly everything, which is fair enough when you're doing that much typing. These two have been circling each other all tournament like a pair of chess players who keep knocking the pieces over.

Then there's Nick, sitting second and absolutely delighted with himself, which is the most dangerous version of Nick. On a football pitch Nick is less a player and more a public health hazard — a six-foot-something blur of mistimed challenges, flying studs and cheerful, weaponised recklessness. His shooting has the arc of a man trying to clear a barn roof from inside the barn. Off the pitch he is tremendous company, mostly because he's loud enough to be heard from the next postcode, which means you always know where the bar is. His main rival is Tom, currently eighth, who we believe is aware of this rivalry and would be very concerned about it if he could work out what time zone he's in. Tom plays psy-trance to fields of people who are, let's say, very open to the experience, and his scouts — if they exist — send reports that are warm, enthusiastic and completely unintelligible. Tom has never won this league. The tournament is nearly over. We're saying nothing.

Sam is fourth, which is genuinely impressive for a man whose primary qualification for fantasy football management is that he once signed a year-long lease on a house because it had its own bar, only to discover on moving day that it had no bathroom. A year. No bathroom. One bar. Sam looked at those empty barrels and made a calculation that tells you everything you need to know about his priorities. He is a Shakespearean man in a fantasy football world, and somehow it's working. His brother Wigs is tenth — counsellor, cricket lover, the gentler O'Shea — and the brothers are separated by forty-two points and what we assume are some very carefully worded family text messages.

Jeremy is fifth. The professor. The man who built the spreadsheet that underpins this entire operation, coded from scratch, a thing of genuine beauty that Tristan still runs off today. Jeremy picks his squad on instinct, taste and accumulated wisdom, the way a sommelier selects a wine — no algorithm, no system, no app. Just knowledge. He watches Lloydy's algorithmic adventures with the quiet, withering expression of a man who has seen this film before and didn't enjoy it the first time. Speaking of which: Chris. Lloydy. Ninth place and the owner of the tournament's most chaotic transfer window, in which his self-built algorithm — his pride, his creation, the thing he built with his own hands in what we can only imagine is a lab full of blinking lights and half-eaten croissants — fired off five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Five. Four years out of date. It was less a transfer engine and more a séance. It did eventually land one valid bid, and that player scored eight points, which Lloydy will tell you vindicates the whole enterprise. It does not.

Nobody was more vindicated by Lloydy's algorithmic chaos than Jake, who has been suspicious of the whole operation since day one and is now insufferable about it in the best possible way. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' he will announce, to no one in particular, before returning to his main business of injuring himself in novel and increasingly improbable ways. The man hurt himself on the way to the dentist. On the way. Not at the dentist, not leaving the dentist — en route, in transit, before a single instrument had been raised. And yet he loves the dentist. Adores the dentist. Would go every week if the journey weren't so statistically perilous. He also spent a significant portion of his transfer budget on Harry Kane, which is a choice, and filled the rest of his squad with players whose combined transfer fee wouldn't cover a round at the bar Jake is currently dancing around, tray aloft, sloshing drinks onto everyone within a three-metre radius. Jake also cannot let go of the recent transfer correction — the one where Jeremy swapped an injured player for a fit one, every other manager nodded along, and Jake alone is still filing a formal grievance in his head. Jeremy has moved on, Jake. The rest of us have moved on. The World Cup has nearly moved on.

Joe S is sixth. Sheerin. The ex-pro. A man of genuine, luminous footballing talent who spent roughly half his playing career horizontal on a treatment table, staring at the ceiling, while the physio made sympathetic noises. The party lifestyle didn't help. The injuries certainly didn't help. The modern technology he still regards with deep, personal suspicion definitely doesn't help his fantasy management, but here he is in sixth, which is either a triumph of instinct or proof that you don't need to understand the platform to beat half the people on it. Dan is seventh, off-grid, smart, quietly dangerous — the kind of manager who says very little and then does something very clever. His main ally is Malik, who is managing from Iceland, which is either a disadvantage or a superpower depending on how you feel about volcanic landscapes and broadband speeds. Malik's squad-building philosophy is simple: if a player has ever worn a Manchester United or Portugal shirt, he is interested. Portugal, of course, have been eliminated. Malik is dealing with this in his own way, from a very long distance.

And finally, Dave. Trossy. Eleventh. Poet, lecturer, lover of puns, player of cymbals on special occasions, and a man who still believes that one day The Culture Show will ring and offer him a segment called Trossy's Wafflecock, in which he will ramble magnificently about cultural nothingness until everyone feels both confused and slightly elevated. Dave is the kind of man who can make a pun out of a team sheet and genuinely mean it as literary criticism. He is also, it should be noted, the kind of man who will read this paragraph very carefully, find the best line in it, and use it at a dinner party within the week. Go on then, Dave. It's yours.

The Final is coming. Two nations left. Thirteen managers with everything still to play for — or, in some cases, everything to play for and a tray of drinks to somehow keep upright. The Morning After will be back when the ball rolls again. Until then: stay well, stay warm, and if you see Jake heading to the dentist, for the love of God, clear a path.
