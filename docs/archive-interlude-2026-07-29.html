THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

Right. There are no fixtures today. The World Cup has reached its final, the last two nations are catching their breath, and for once there is absolutely nothing to report. No goals, no blanks, no last-minute heartbreaks to narrate. Which means we have to talk about you lot. Pull up a chair. This is the bit where the football stops and the roasting begins — a warm, affectionate, entirely merited tour of the thirteen human beings who have been shouting at a spreadsheet for the past few weeks. Settle in.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a 91-point lead, and he would very much like you to know that this is the result of careful, considered management. It is not. It is the result of a decent transfer window, a bit of luck, and the fact that Joe spends roughly forty percent of his waking life trying to get AI tools to do what they claim they can do — a crusade that has never once fully succeeded and yet continues with the dedication of a man who believes, genuinely believes, that this next prompt will be the one. The irony that he is being read about in a document generated by an AI that also cannot keep its promises is noted, filed, and left there.

Meanwhile, Tristan — third, 97 points back, furious — is doing what Tristan does, which is manually combing the FIFA website for data with the grim, methodical energy of a man who does not trust machines, does not trust shortcuts, and does not, frankly, trust Joe A's lead to hold. He is probably right on at least one of those. He throws his hands up in disgust approximately six times per matchday. By the final, that number will be higher. Sitting between them at second is Nick, who has 370 points and a shooting technique best described as 'agricultural'. On a football pitch Nick is a six-foot-something wrecking ball who flies into tackles with the cheerful recklessness of a man who has never once considered the structural integrity of another person's ankles. His shots go approximately fifteen feet over the crossbar. He is, somehow, second in the league. Nobody mention it to him or he'll celebrate by taking someone out.

Fourth is Sam, and Sam deserves a moment. He is an expressive, brilliant, Shakespearean-adjacent man who loves beer and music and cricket and his own face — which, to be fair to the team name, is extraordinarily expressive. Sam once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar, only to discover on moving-in day that the property contained no bathroom whatsoever. He looked at the empty bar barrels. He considered his options. He is still, we understand, considering them. Right next to him at fifth is his brother Wigs, a counsellor of genuine warmth and wit, who has spent the entire tournament gently processing everyone else's fantasy football grief while quietly accumulating 319 points in tenth. The O'Shea brothers, separated by 42 points and a lifetime of sibling energy. Sam would like Wigs to know he is coming for him. Wigs would like Sam to know he has heard this before.

Sixth place belongs to Joe S — the Sheerin, the ex-pro, the man whose actual football career was spent approximately half on the pitch and half horizontal on a physio's treatment table. A supremely gifted player who simply could not stay upright long enough to prove it consistently. He is baffled by most technology made after 1997, beloved by everyone who has ever met him, and currently managing a fantasy team from what we can only assume is a position of cheerful, party-fuelled instinct. He would have been higher, but something probably went wrong with his hamstring just reading the fixtures.

Seventh is Dan, off-grid, sharp, historically excellent at this game, and currently allied with Malik at the bottom of the table in thirteenth. Malik — our quirky, brilliant Icelandic correspondent — manages Propaganda Parade from a considerable geographical distance and with a very specific transfer philosophy: if a player has ever worn a Manchester United or Portugal shirt, Malik wants him. Portugal, of course, have been eliminated. This is not ideal for a man whose entire scouting network is essentially 'did Cristiano once nod at him in a tunnel?' Dan, his ally, is doing considerably better at seventh and is too smart to say so out loud.

Eighth is Tom, 321 points, who is probably fine. Tom is always fine. Ninth — one point behind — is Chris, aka Lloydy, who is emphatically not fine, because Chris's AI algorithm — his pride, his project, his contribution to the cutting edge of fantasy football science — recently held the entire league's transfer window hostage while it fired off five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Five. In a row. Before finally, mercifully, landing on someone current, who at least went and scored 8 points. Jake, in twelfth, has not stopped talking about this. Jake — renewable energy manager, wind turbine enthusiast, devoted dental patient, man who once injured himself on the way to the dentist — is loudly, paranoidly, magnificently vindicated. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' he announces, to no one in particular, before speculating at length about what else Lloydy's algorithm is doing in his private office complex when nobody's watching. Jake's Harry Kane, meanwhile, is sitting on a points total that does not justify the budget. The Kane debate continues. It will always continue.

That leaves Jeremy at fifth and Dave at eleventh. Jeremy — the professor, the man who built the spreadsheet that runs this entire operation — picks his team purely on instinct, taste and experience, and has never once asked a machine to do it for him. He could build an algorithm in an afternoon. He simply chooses not to, and watches Lloydy's chaos with the quiet, withering expression of a man who knew. Dave, meanwhile, is a poet and lecturer with 300 points, a love of wordplay, and a long-held ambition to host a segment on The Culture Show called Trossy's Wafflecock, in which he would discourse at length on cultural nothingness until everyone present had been thoroughly, warmly, meaninglessly enlightened. He plays the cymbals. Only on special occasions. This, perhaps, is one.

The Final is coming. One match. One winner. Thirteen managers, one trophy, and a points gap that is large but not, as Tristan would remind you while manually refreshing the FIFA site, insurmountable. Jake will shout his team name at least twice before kick-off. Someone will get injured — statistically, Joe S or Jake, possibly both. The algorithm will be watching. The professor will not need it. See you on the other side.
