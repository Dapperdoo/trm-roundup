THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

Good morning. No fixtures. Not a single one. The World Cup has reached that strange, breathless pause before the Final, and rather than stare at an empty results page, we thought we'd turn the spotlight where it truly belongs: on the thirteen people who have been shouting at spreadsheets and refreshing the FIFA website since June. Pull up a chair. This is your managers. All of them. In one room. God help us all.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting there at 461 points and he'd like you to know it. He won't say it directly — he'll just be quietly, cheerfully present, like a man who's won a pub quiz and is pretending not to have noticed the scoreboard. Joe spends a meaningful portion of his waking life trying to get AI tools to do what they promise. He has been at war with these things for months. He feeds them prompts, they hallucinate. He asks for a summary, they invent one. He requests a table, they return a poem. And yet here he is, top of the league, having assembled a squad largely by hand, with a Guinness nearby and a vague sense that it'll probably be fine. The real joke is that his lead is genuinely under pressure now, with Tristan breathing down his neck at third. Tristan — big, Russian-raised, London-sharpened, does NOT suffer fools — has been gleaning every single data point by hand from the FIFA site, squinting at the screen, cross-referencing Jez's spreadsheet, and throwing his hands up in disgust at roughly forty-minute intervals. The two of them are locked in a rivalry that is essentially: organised fury versus cheerful chaos. Both are, somehow, working.

Nick is second. Nick, who on a football pitch is less a player and more a public hazard — six foot something of reckless, mistimed, frequently deliberate catastrophe, flying into challenges like a man who has confused a five-a-side match with a war crime. His shooting is the stuff of legend: a wild, full-body golf swing that sends the ball somewhere between the crossbar and low Earth orbit. And yet: 370 points. Second place. His main rival is Tom, currently eighth on 321, which would sting more if Tom were in a position to feel it. Last confirmed sighting of Tom, he was somewhere between a field and a flight, radiating happiness and making absolutely no sense to anyone nearby. One eyewitness report, relayed third-hand, described him as 'very enthusiastic about something, possibly a country.' He is fine. He is always fine. He has, however, never won this league, and the gap to Nick is not closing.

Fourth place belongs to Sam — actor, dancer, Shakespeare devotee, cricket man, and a person whose face is, genuinely, its own entire weather system. Sam is the kind of man who could convey the complete works of Chekhov with a single eyebrow. He is also, it must be said, a man who once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had a bar, only to discover on moving day that the property contained no bathroom whatsoever. He stood there, surrounded by boxes, staring at the empty bar barrels, doing the maths. His brother Wigs — counsellor, cricketer, tenth place, gentle giant of the group — was presumably on the phone offering emotional support. Wigs is the kind of person who makes everyone feel heard, which is a wonderful quality, and also means he has to listen to Sam's stories, which is a cross to bear.

Fifth is Jeremy. The Professor. The man who built the spreadsheet that holds this entire league together, coded from scratch, still running. Jeremy picks his team on instinct, taste and experience — no algorithm, no system, no AI. He could build one. He simply doesn't, and he is quietly, devastatingly unimpressed by those who do. He watches the chaos of the transfer window with the expression of a Swiss watchmaker observing someone fix a clock with a hammer. On the five-a-side pitch he is a niggling, precise menace — not reckless like Nick, just relentlessly, irritatingly there, nicking the ball off you at exactly the wrong moment. Sixth is Joe S, Back of the Van United, beloved by all, a genuinely gifted footballer whose career was spent approximately forty percent on the pitch and sixty percent in the physio room, horizontal, staring at the ceiling, wondering what he'd done wrong. Joe S could dribble past four men and then pull a hamstring celebrating. He is baffled by most technology manufactured after 2003 and remains, somehow, one of the most popular men in any room he enters.

Seventh is Dan, off-grid, sharp as a tack, historically one of the best fantasy managers in this league, and currently allied with Malik — the quirky, brilliant Icelander managing from somewhere in the North Atlantic, who has built his squad on a single, unwavering principle: if they have ever worn a Manchester United or Portugal shirt, they are in. Portugal, of course, have been eliminated. This has not visibly shaken Malik's philosophy. Dan, for his part, is smart enough not to say anything about it. That's what allies are for.

Which brings us to Chris — Lloydy — ninth, 320 points, never sitting still, always building something, biking somewhere, rewiring something that probably didn't need rewiring. Chris's algorithm. We don't need to relitigate the whole saga, but: five bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Five. The man built a transfer engine and it came back holding a séance. It was trying to sign ghosts. It held up the entire league's window while it communed with the class of four years ago. One bid eventually landed — a valid, current player — who did at least score 8 points, which is the algorithm's sole redemption arc. Jake — twelfth, 292 points, renewable energy project manager, lover of wind turbines, mushrooms, and the dentist — has not moved on. Jake LOVES the dentist. He will tell you about his last appointment unprompted. He injured himself on the way to his last appointment, which is very on-brand, because Jake injures himself doing essentially nothing: walking, sitting, existing in space. He also still cannot let go of the transfer-window business with Jeremy — every other manager quietly accepted that an honest mistake was honestly corrected, and Jake alone remains in the courtroom, presenting evidence. In the meantime he is on the dancefloor, holding a full tray of drinks, shouting 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' at no one in particular, and the drinks are already going.

At the bottom, eleventh, is Dave — Trossy Ginge — poet, lecturer, cymbal player (on special occasions only), and a man who dreams of hosting a segment on The Culture Show called 'Trossy's Wafflecock', in which he would ramble about cultural nothingness with great sincerity and considerable wordplay. Dave is on 300 points and has a pun ready for every occasion, which is either a gift or a warning, depending on how much you've had to drink. He is currently on a city break somewhere, eating something interesting, smoking, and composing a couplet about the tournament that will be either brilliant or completely impenetrable. Possibly both.

That's your thirteen. The Final is coming — one match, one winner, and then we find out who's been right all along. The points table will have its say. The algorithm will presumably have an opinion. Jake will shout his team name. And somewhere, a tray of drinks is already in danger. See you on the other side.
