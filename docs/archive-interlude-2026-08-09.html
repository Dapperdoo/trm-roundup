THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football. Just thirteen people to mock.

Good morning, and welcome to a very special edition of The Morning After. There are no fixtures today. None. The World Cup has paused, the pitches are empty, the VAR officials are finally getting some sleep, and we are left with nothing but ourselves and our choices. So. In the absence of actual football to report, we are turning the spotlight where it truly belongs: on the thirteen individuals who have spent the last several weeks shouting at spreadsheets, refreshing the FIFA website, and making decisions of genuinely questionable quality. Ladies and gentlemen — your managers.

Let's start at the top, because someone has to. Joe A sits first in this league with 461 points, which is a comfortable lead and which Joe A will tell you is the result of careful planning, smart recruitment and a clear tactical vision. It is not. It is the result of a few decent signings and the fact that his rivals have spent the tournament making slightly worse decisions than he has. Joe A spends most of his waking hours locked in furious, one-sided combat with AI tools that promise to revolutionise his workflow and then immediately fail to do anything they claimed. He has not yet won. The AI has not yet delivered. He is, however, still going. We respect the commitment. Meanwhile, Tristan — third, 364 points, breathing down his neck — is doing his own version of the same thing, except Tristan's method involves manually clicking through the FIFA website one page at a time, harvesting every number by hand like a man picking blackberries in a field. He throws his hands up in disgust at approximately everything. Tristan, the numbers are right there. Joe A, the AI is not your friend. Between the two of them they have invented a rivalry that is essentially: one man fighting a computer, and another man refusing to use one.

Elsewhere in the top half we find Nick in second place, which is extraordinary and should not be allowed. Nick on a football pitch is a public safety concern — a six-foot-something wrecking ball who launches himself into tackles with the accuracy of a man attempting a golf shot in a hurricane, ball nowhere near him, opponent briefly airborne, referee reaching for a pocket. His shooting makes it worse. The ball goes up. It keeps going up. It clears the bar, clears the stand, and is last seen somewhere over the car park. That Nick is second in this league is either proof that fantasy football rewards boldness, or proof that fantasy football is broken. Possibly both. His main rival is Tom, who is eighth, and who — wherever he currently is — is absolutely fine about it.

Let's talk about Jake. Jake is twelfth. Jake is also, right now, the most emotionally activated person in this entire league, and that is a crowded field. Jake hurt himself recently — we don't have the full details, we never do, but the working theory is that it happened en route somewhere entirely benign, because that is how Jake's body works. The man once sustained an injury on his way to the dentist. Speaking of which: Jake loves the dentist. Genuinely, sincerely, with a warmth he does not extend to most humans. He will tell you about his last appointment unprompted. He will tell you it was brilliant. Somewhere in a waiting room, a hygienist has no idea she has a superfan. Jake also carries two grudges around like hand luggage. The first: Jeremy, in the recent transfer window, swapped out an injured player Jake had bought and replaced him with a fit one. Every other manager shrugged and moved on. Jake has not moved on. Jake will not move on. The second: Chris. Lloydy's algorithm — the one that fired five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup before accidentally landing a valid one — has confirmed every paranoid suspicion Jake has ever held about AI, about Chris, and about what Chris is doing in that office complex of his when nobody is watching. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' Jake will shout, to no one in particular, reminding himself and the room that he exists and his team exists and he is still here. He is. He's twelfth. But he's still here.

Chris, for his part, is ninth, and has the decency to look slightly embarrassed about the algorithm, which is the correct response. The lab has been quiet since the window closed. Whether that is because the system has been fixed, or because it is currently attempting to sign Miroslav Klose, we cannot say. Jeremy — fifth, 360 points, the man who built the spreadsheet this entire league runs on — watches all of this with the quiet, withering patience of someone who could build a better algorithm before breakfast and has chosen, on principle, never to do so. Jeremy picks his team on instinct, taste and experience. He always has. He is not going to start asking a machine.

Down at the bottom we find Malik, thirteenth, managing from Iceland with characteristic serenity and a transfer policy that can be summarised as: has he played for Manchester United? Has he played for Portugal? Then he'll do. Portugal, of course, are eliminated. This has not visibly troubled Malik, who has Dan — seventh, his closest ally — to commiserate with. Dan lives off-grid, which in this context means he is probably the only manager in the league who genuinely doesn't care about his league position, and also the only one who could survive if the grid went down tomorrow. They are an unlikely pair. They are very good friends.

Then there is Dave, eleventh, who has processed his league position through the lens of a man who sees poetry in all things, including mid-table mediocrity. Dave would like you to know that his team's struggles are, in a sense, a meditation on the nature of ambition itself — a Wafflecock segment waiting to happen, if only The Culture Show would return his calls. He plays the cymbals. Only on special occasions. This has been one of them. Wigs is tenth, which puts him one place and one point above Dave, a gap that will mean absolutely nothing to either of them because they are both fundamentally decent people who are enjoying the tournament regardless. Wigs counsels. Wigs listens. Wigs is the kind of person who makes a room feel calmer just by being in it. His brother Sam is fourth — Look at his face, just look at his FACE — and is almost certainly celebrating this fact somewhere theatrical, possibly with a pint, possibly quoting Shakespeare at someone who did not ask, possibly on a dancefloor. Sam once signed a year-long lease on a house because it had its own bar, only to discover it had no bathroom. He made it work. That is Sam in a sentence.

Which brings us to Joe S, sixth, Back of the Van United, a man who was — by all accounts, by everyone who saw him play — a genuinely brilliant footballer. Gifted. Flair. The full package. He was also, for roughly half his career, horizontal on a treatment table while someone prodded his hamstring. The injuries were relentless, creative, occasionally implausible. Joe S has more physio room anecdotes than match anecdotes, and the match anecdotes are extraordinary. He is now baffled by his phone. This is fine. He is universally beloved and sixth in the league, and both of those things are entirely deserved.

That's your lot. Thirteen managers, no football, and entirely too much self-knowledge in one room. The World Cup Final is on the horizon — one match left, one winner, and thirteen fantasy squads about to find out whether any of this was worth it. Get some rest. Hydrate. Jake, book a dentist appointment — you've earned it, and at least you'll be safe in the chair.
