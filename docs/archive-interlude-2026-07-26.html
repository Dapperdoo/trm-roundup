THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

Good morning. There are no fixtures today — the World Cup has reached its final and is holding its breath before the last act. Which means the football can wait, and we turn the spotlight squarely onto the thirteen people who have been shouting at their phones for the past several weeks. Pull up a chair. This is the room. Let's work it.

We should start at the top, because Joe A is sitting there at 461 points and he'd like you to know that. He wouldn't say it himself — he's too busy trying to get an AI tool to do something it promised it could do three weeks ago and still hasn't. That's Joe's actual hobby: not football, not Guinness, not the cheerful domestic chaos he calls a flat — it's the sisyphean crusade of making technology keep its word. He's essentially running a one-man consumer-rights campaign against the entire AI industry, one broken chatbot at a time. Meanwhile his fantasy team sits first in the league, largely unattended, like a houseplant that thrived because someone forgot to overwater it. Tristan, in third, is watching this with barely concealed fury, cross-referencing the FIFA website by hand, entry by entry, doing everything the hard way — and still a hundred points back. The injustice is not lost on him. His hands go up. They always go up.

Nick is second, which is almost more alarming than Joe A being first. This is a man who, on a football pitch, plays like a wrecking ball that's developed opinions. The tackles aren't mistimed — they're deliberate. They're a statement. He goes in late, he goes in high, he goes in with the energy of someone who has decided that the laws of the game are more of a guideline. Off the pitch he's the loudest man in any room — great, booming voice, enormous laugh — and his fantasy team reflects this: big, blunt, unapologetic. His main rival is Tom, who is currently eighth and, if we're being honest, may not have checked the standings recently. Tom's relationship with this league exists somewhere between 'deeply invested' and 'I'll look at it when I land'. He has never won it, which is a fact that deserves to sit in the room for a moment before we all move on.

Jeremy is fifth — the professor, the man who built the spreadsheet that underpins this entire operation, who could automate every decision in his sleep and chooses, deliberately, not to. He picks his team on instinct and taste, the way you'd choose a wine or a poem. He watches Lloydy's algorithm saga with the quiet, withering expression of a surgeon watching someone try to perform an appendectomy with a spanner. Speaking of which: Chris. Ninth. The algorithm that was going to change everything instead fired off five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup — players who, in some cases, have since retired, moved on, or simply aged four years past relevance. It held the entire league's transfer window hostage before finally, mercifully, landing one valid bid. That player scored 8 points. Chris would like you to focus on that last part. Jake would like you to focus on literally everything else.

Jake. Twelfth. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' Yes, thank you, Jake. He's been saying it for weeks and it still makes him jump slightly when he hears it back. He overspent catastrophically on Harry Kane — a decision he defends with the energy of a man who has never once considered that he might be wrong — and filled the rest of the squad with players whose transfer fees could be covered by a generous tip. He also, at some point between the transfer window and now, injured himself. The details are unclear. They are always unclear. What is known is that Jake's body treats ordinary life as an extreme sport: he once hurt himself on the way to the dentist, which he then attended anyway because Jake loves the dentist. Genuinely loves it. Talks about it. The man views a check-up the way other people view a spa day. He is currently nursing a fresh grudge against Jeremy — who, a couple of days ago, corrected an honest transfer mistake by swapping an injured player for a fit one, a move every other manager shrugged off in about four seconds. Jake has not shrugged. Jake will not shrug. Jake is keeping receipts. He is also, somewhere this evening, on a dancefloor with a full tray of drinks, and someone nearby is about to get very wet.

Sam is fourth — respectable, expressive, theatrical, the kind of man who approaches a fantasy football league the way he'd approach a Shakespeare production: with enormous feeling and occasional structural chaos. His brother Wigs is tenth, which means the family reunion at Christmas will have a very clear pecking order. Wigs, for his part, is warm and funny and will absolutely let it go. Sam will not let it go. He will monologue about it. Dave is eleventh — Trossy Ginge, the poet-lecturer who finds meaning in wordplay and puns and has never quite abandoned the dream of presenting a segment on The Culture Show in which he talks, at length, about nothing in particular, with great sincerity. The segment is called Trossy's Wafflecock. It would be unmissable. Commission it.

Down at the bottom, Malik is thirteenth — managing from Iceland, signing anyone who has ever pulled on a Manchester United or Portugal shirt, which is an increasingly narrow pool now that Portugal are out of the tournament. His ally Dan is seventh, living off-grid, probably aware of the standings through some means that doesn't involve the internet. Dan is smart enough to be doing better and witty enough not to care that he isn't, which is a very specific kind of composure. And then there's Joe S — sixth, Back of the Van United — the most naturally gifted footballer in this room, a man whose playing career was spent in roughly equal parts on the pitch and on the treatment table. He is universally beloved. He is also, right now, trying to figure out how to open a PDF, and we should leave him to it with love.

The Final is coming. One match left in this entire tournament — one last set of points to be carved out, one last chance for the table to lurch. Joe A's lead looks comfortable until you remember that football has a sense of humour, and so does this league. See you on the other side.
