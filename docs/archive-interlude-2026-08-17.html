THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football. Just thirteen managers. God help us.

There are no fixtures today. The World Cup has paused for breath — one match left to play, the Final itself — and so, with nothing to actually report, we are turning the spotlight away from the pitch and directly onto the thirteen people who have been shouting at it for the past several weeks. Consider this the after-dinner speech nobody asked for. Settle in.

Let's start at the top, because someone has to. Joe A sits first in the table with 461 points, which is genuinely impressive, and he would like you to know that he is not resting on his laurels. He is, in fact, currently trying to get an AI tool to summarise his laurels for him, and it keeps hallucinating laurels he doesn't have. He's been at it for three hours. The Guinness is warm. The AI has now confidently told him he finished second. He's refreshed the page fourteen times. This is how Joe A spends his leisure time — not relaxing, not watching football, but locked in a low-grade war of attrition with software that keeps almost working. Tristan is right behind him in third, by the way, and gaining.

Speaking of Tristan: big man, sweet tooth, absolutely zero tolerance for nonsense. He runs his entire operation off Jez's spreadsheet, cross-referencing every single number by hand from the FIFA website like a Soviet-era archivist who doesn't trust the photocopier. It takes him twice as long as everyone else and he is twice as accurate. He has been known to throw his hands up in disgust at a single misplaced decimal. He will not be throwing his hands up at third place, but he will absolutely be throwing them up at the 97-point gap to Joe A, which he is quietly, methodically, furiously closing.

Now, Jeremy — fifth, 360 points, and the man who built the very spreadsheet Tristan is squinting at. A brilliant programmer. Could, if he chose, construct an algorithm that would run this league from a server farm. Instead he picks his team the way a sommelier picks a wine: instinct, experience, a faint look of disdain for anyone who needs a chart to tell them what they already know. He has watched Lloydy's algorithm fire five bids for players from the 2022 World Cup with the expression of a man watching someone use a sundial to book a flight. He has said nothing. He doesn't need to. The eyebrow does the work.

Which brings us to Chris. Lloydy. Ninth. The mad scientist in the lab, the man whose AI transfer engine briefly became a time machine — tunnelling back to Qatar 2022, rattling off bids for players who have since retired, aged out, or simply moved on with their lives. Five bids. Five. Before it accidentally stumbled onto someone valid. The player scored 8 points, which Lloydy has been dining out on ever since, as though a stopped clock had correctly predicted a lunar eclipse. Jake, meanwhile — twelfth, still seething — has been vindicated so completely that he has started shouting 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' at the ceiling just to celebrate. Jake injured himself on the way to the dentist last month. He is still the saner man in this particular argument.

Jake. Oh, Jake. Let's give him a moment. He loves wind turbines. He loves mushrooms. He loves the dentist — genuinely, sincerely, with a warmth most people reserve for family members. He will describe a particularly clean scale-and-polish the way others describe a holiday. He is also, somehow, the only person in this league who managed to hurt himself in transit to a routine dental appointment, which tells you everything about the Jake Injury Cinematic Universe. He overspent enormously on Harry Kane. He still shouts his own team name unprompted. And he cannot — will not — let go of the transfer grievance from a couple of days ago, in which Jeremy corrected an honest mistake and every other manager shrugged and moved on. Jake has not moved on. Jake is still there. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' he shouts, into the middle distance, at no one.

Over on the injury ward, Joe S is in sixth with 338 points and a physio bill that could fund a mid-table Championship club for a season. The man was a genuinely gifted footballer — the kind who made it look effortless — and spent approximately half his career making it look effortless from a treatment table. He is now baffled by the group chat. He has sent three voice notes trying to ask what a 'transfer window' is. He is beloved by everyone in this league, which is lucky, because he is going to need help navigating the app.

Then there's the cultural wing of the operation. Dave — eleventh, 300 points, lecturer, poet, lover of wordplay — is somewhere composing a sestina about a defensive midfielder's xG. He plays the cymbals, but only on special occasions, which raises more questions than it answers. He still dreams of hosting 'Trossy's Wafflecock' on The Culture Show: a segment in which he would say a great many words about a great many things and mean, in the end, something very close to nothing, but with tremendous conviction. Sam — fourth, 361 points, and looking extremely expressive about it — is his brother Wigs's brother, which is the most efficient way to describe both of them. Sam once signed a year-long lease on a house because it had its own bar, moved in, and discovered it had no bathroom. He is a Shakespeare man. He is a cricket man. He is a man who has, on at least one occasion, lost both shoes simultaneously on a night out. He is fourth in this league and he deserves every point.

Wigs is tenth, counselling everyone through it. Tom is eighth, and we will simply say he is somewhere, doing something, and leave it at that. Nick is second — an extraordinary 370 points — and is presumably celebrating by launching a shot approximately forty yards over the crossbar and then looking absolutely baffled as to why it didn't go in. Dan is seventh, off-grid and unbothered, allied with Malik in thirteenth, who is last in the table and still, somewhere in Iceland, scanning the Portugal squad list for anyone he might have missed. He has not missed anyone. He has all of them. Portugal are eliminated. This is fine.

The Final awaits. One match. Thirteen managers. Whatever happens on that pitch, the standings will shift, scores will land, and The Morning After will be back with actual football to report — goals, points, glory, and the inevitable disaster that befalls whoever least deserves it. Until then: go home, get some rest, and Jake — step away from the transfer tab.

The World Cup Final is the only fixture left. When it's done, this league has a winner. We'll see you on the other side.
