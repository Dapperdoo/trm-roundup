THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

Right, there are no fixtures today. The World Cup has paused, the pitches are resting, and for once nobody is blanking, nobody is hauling, and nobody's goalkeeper is conceding a soft one in the 94th minute. Which means we have nothing to talk about except the thirteen people who have been sweating over this league since the first whistle. Pull up a chair. This is going to be affectionate. Mostly.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a nine-point lead, and he would like you to know that his squad is a product of careful research, shrewd judgement, and a forensic eye for value. What he will not tell you is that he has spent approximately forty hours of his life this tournament trying to get an AI tool to do something — anything — it actually promised it could do, and every single attempt has ended with him staring at a screen that has confidently produced the wrong thing in a very convincing font. He's still winning the league, which is either an argument for human ingenuity or an argument that the AI was at least keeping him too busy to make bad transfers. Tristan, his closest rival, is three points back in third and absolutely furious about it — not loudly furious, just the quiet, hands-thrown-up-in-disgust kind of furious that is somehow worse. Tristan gleans every single number by hand from the FIFA website, which is the fantasy-football equivalent of doing your tax return in pencil, and he is STILL nearly catching Joe A. That should bother Joe A more than it does.

Sandwiched between them in second is Nick, who has absolutely no business being there and yet here we are. Nick plays football like a man who has been told the rules but has chosen to interpret them as suggestions. The shooting — the wild, arcing, golf-swing shooting that clears the crossbar and lands somewhere in the car park — is one thing. The tackling is another. Nick doesn't so much tackle as launch himself at a general area of the pitch and hope for the best, and the best is rarely what arrives. He is very tall, very loud, and currently second in the league, which is the sporting equivalent of a wrecking ball winning a pottery competition. Tom, his rival, is down in eighth on 321 and has no comment because Tom is — look, we don't know exactly where Tom is right now, but there is a strong chance it involves a field, a sound system, and someone nearby who cannot fully explain what they are experiencing.

Sam is fourth, which is impressive for a man who once signed a year-long lease on a house because it had its own bar, moved in, and discovered there was no bathroom. No bathroom. A full year. The bar was real; the plumbing was not. Sam is a man of the theatre, of Shakespeare, of cricket, of beer and music and wit — and he made a completely reasonable housing decision that any of us might have made, if we were Sam, which most of us are not equipped to be. His brother Wigs is tenth, which means the family is split across the table like a Shakespearean subplot. Wigs is a counsellor, which is useful, because someone in this league is going to need one by the time the final is played.

Jeremy is fifth on 360, quietly, precisely, exactly where a man of his calibre should be. He built the spreadsheet this entire league runs on. He could, if he wanted, build an algorithm to pick his team. He has chosen not to, because Jeremy picks on instinct, taste and experience — the organic approach — and he watches Lloydy's algorithmic adventures with the expression of a master craftsman watching someone try to assemble furniture using a leaf blower. Speaking of which: Chris. Lloydy. Ninth on 320, and the recent transfer window remains a thing. His algorithm — his pride, his project, his mad-scientist baby — fired off five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Five. Before eventually landing on one valid signing who did, to be fair, score eight points. The algorithm built a time machine. It was trying to sign ghosts. And Portugal is now eliminated, which means half those 2022 phantoms it was chasing have now joined the current tournament's eliminated list too. The bot is, if anything, ahead of the curve.

Jake. Oh, Jake. Twelfth on 292, and carrying more grievances than points. He injured himself — and we must stress this, because it bears repeating — on the way to the dentist. Not at the dentist. Not leaving the dentist. On the way there. Jake's body treats routine journeys as extreme sports. He also spent a significant portion of his transfer budget on Harry Kane, and Harry Kane's Portugal have been eliminated, so that's that chapter closed. But Jake is fine. Jake is always fine. He will shout 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' into a room apropos of nothing, he will take to the dancefloor holding a full tray of drinks, he will distribute those drinks across everyone within a three-metre radius, and he will do all of this while still, somehow, nursing a grudge about a transfer correction that Jeremy made — correctly, honestly, transparently — a couple of days ago, which every other manager accepted and moved on from, and which Jake has filed under 'unresolved business' alongside his feelings about Lloydy's AI and the general injustice of the universe.

Dan is seventh on 329, living off-grid, thinking clearly, allied with Malik who is propping up the table in thirteenth on 280 and signing players on the basis that they have, at some point, worn a Manchester United or Portugal shirt. Portugal are out. This is not ideal for Malik's recruitment philosophy, though it does narrow the field considerably, which might actually speed up his decision-making. Dan will probably have a wry observation about all of this. Dan usually does. Meanwhile Dave — Trossy, Trossy Ginge — is eleventh on 300, a poet and lecturer who plays the cymbals only on special occasions and still dreams of hosting a segment on The Culture Show in which he talks about cultural nothingness with great sincerity and absolutely no resolution. He would call it 'Trossy's Wafflecock'. The BBC has not yet called. Dave remains hopeful. Dave loves a pun, loves a city break, loves a beer, and is currently three points off Wigs, which is the kind of gap a well-placed wordplay could close if wordplay counted for anything in this league, which it does not.

And then there is Joe S — sixth on 338, Back of the Van United, universally beloved, genuinely one of the most talented footballers any of us has ever watched — when he was actually on the pitch, which was, statistically, less often than the treatment table. Joe S's career was a masterclass in almost. Almost fit. Almost back. Almost — oh, he's pulled his hamstring again. He loved the game and the game loved him back, it just also kept breaking him into pieces. He is baffled by most technology and would like someone to explain what is happening on his phone. He is also, quietly, sixth in the league and very much in this. Which is the most Joe S thing possible: effortlessly, charmingly, inexplicably still in contention despite everything.

The next fixtures will come. Nations will play, players will score or blank, managers will celebrate or despair, and Jake will probably injure himself reading the fixture list. Until then — this is your Morning After, with nothing to report and everything to say.
