THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. So let's talk about you lot.

Good morning. No fixtures today — the tournament pauses for breath before the final, and so, with nothing to actually report, we turn the spotlight where it arguably belongs: on the thirteen people who have spent the last several weeks shouting at spreadsheets, refreshing the FIFA website, and making increasingly questionable life decisions in the name of fantasy football. Ladies and gentlemen, your managers.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a one-hundred-point lead, and he would like you to know that this is the result of careful, considered management. Joe A would also like his AI tools to actually do what they say on the tin, but that's a separate ongoing tragedy. The man spends genuinely alarming portions of his free time trying to get large language models to honour their promises, which is — and I say this with all the warmth in the world — a bit like hiring a plumber who keeps describing pipes in extraordinary detail but won't touch the actual leak. His lead is real. His methods are chaotic. His Guinness consumption is, by all accounts, structural. Tristan, sitting third on 364 and glowering from just off-stage, has been hand-keying data from the FIFA website one row at a time like a man transcribing the Domesday Book, and he is absolutely convinced this diligence will be rewarded. It will not be rewarded fast enough, Tristan. The gap is a hundred points. You may need to throw your hands up.

Speaking of data infrastructure: a round of applause for Jeremy, fifth on 360, who built the spreadsheet that underpins this entire operation from scratch, picks his team on pure instinct and taste like a man choosing wine by the label, and has watched the AI discourse unfold around him with the quiet, withering expression of a professor who set the exam and already knows everyone's going to fail it. Jeremy does not need an algorithm. Jeremy IS the algorithm. He just runs it in his head, and it has manners. Meanwhile, Chris — ninth, 320 points, Lloydy himself — is down in his lab doing God knows what. The algorithm that held up the entire league's transfer window by submitting five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup has, we are told, been 'recalibrated.' Portugal are eliminated, by the way. Several of those 2022 bids are now doubly historical.

Now. Jake. Twelfth on 292, and — Snacob's Ladder! — still going. Jake, who overspent catastrophically on Harry Kane and filled the remaining slots with men whose names autocorrect doesn't recognise, is nevertheless a man of tremendous energy. He injured himself recently. We don't know exactly how or when, but the smart money is on 'travelling to or from a dental appointment,' because Jake has the injury record of a man who pulls a hamstring opening the fridge and the dental enthusiasm of a man who considers a check-up a treat. He is also still furious — still, days later, still — about Jeremy adjusting a transfer that every other manager shrugged off in about four seconds. Every other manager. Jake alone has filed this under Grievances, Capital G, and will be reopening the file at a moment's notice. He also suspects Lloydy is doing something sinister with AI across his entire office complex. Jake, mate. Lloydy's algorithm couldn't find a player from this decade. It's fine.

Let us take a moment for the walking wounded. Joe S — sixth, 338 points, universally beloved, Back of the Van United — had a career of such dazzling promise and such relentless physical catastrophe that his physio probably has a loyalty card with his name on it. The man could turn an ankle reading a menu. He is baffled by most technology made after 1997 and has the energy of someone who has genuinely lived, which is to say: a lot of late nights, a lot of charm, and the occasional morning where the details are impressionistic. Sam, fourth on 361 — Look at his face, just LOOK at it — is his spiritual counterpart: a stage and screen performer of genuine wit and scattiness who once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar, only to discover the property contained no bathroom whatsoever. Sam stood in that empty bar on moving-in day, surrounded by barrels, and made a decision about priorities that tells you everything about him. He is also a Shakespeare man, which means he has the vocabulary to describe his fantasy season in iambic pentameter and the wisdom to know it wouldn't help.

Brothers, now. Wigs — tenth, 319, 50 Shades of O'Shea — is the gentler, counselling half of the Sam-and-Wigs operation: warm, witty, the sort of person who actually listens when you talk, which in this league makes him a statistical outlier. He and Sam share a love of cricket, which means they are both capable of watching something for five days and calling it entertainment. Dave, eleventh on 300, is down the bottom of the table but spiritually very much in the middle of a long, winding sentence about culture. Trossy's Giants. Dave is a lecturer, a poet, a man who plays the cymbals on special occasions and who dreams — genuinely dreams — of hosting a segment on The Culture Show called 'Trossy's Wafflecock,' in which he would hold forth on cultural nothingness with great sincerity. Dave, the table may be against you, but the pitch is yours.

Up in the middle of the pack: Tom, eighth on 321, Anamaduwa Athletic, is somewhere. Genuinely unclear where. A source was reached for comment and said, and I quote: 'the colours are very loud and the rice is good and I think it's Tuesday.' Tom plays psy-trance and only psy-trance, and somewhere out there a field of people with very dilated pupils are having the time of their lives while Tom's fantasy team quietly ticks over. His main rival is Nick, second on 370 and the highest genuine threat to Joe A's throne. Nick is very tall, very loud, and on a football pitch an absolute menace — the kind of player who goes into a tackle like he's driving a golf ball and leaves three opponents in a heap while the ball rolls gently out for a throw-in. His shooting is majestic in its ambition and catastrophic in its execution. Nick, second place. Genuinely dangerous. Do not get in his way, literally or figuratively.

Finally: Dan, seventh on 329, living off-grid, smart, alternative, historically excellent at this, quietly allied with Malik — thirteenth on 280, managing from Iceland, signing anyone who has ever pulled on a Manchester United or Portugal shirt. Portugal, as mentioned, are out. Malik's transfer policy has just lost its second pillar. Dan and Malik are allies in the way that two people in a lifeboat are allies: affectionate, mutually supportive, and both very aware of the water.

The final is coming. One match left in this entire tournament, and thirteen managers with everything still to play for in the table. Rest up, check your squads, and — Jake — maybe take a different route to the dentist this time. The Morning After will be back when the football is.
