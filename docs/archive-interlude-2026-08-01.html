THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

Right. There are no fixtures today. The tournament is down to its final handful of nations, half the world has been eliminated, and for one merciful day the scoreboard goes dark. Which means we turn the spotlight away from the players — and directly onto the thirteen human beings who have been yelling at a spreadsheet for the past few weeks. Pull up a chair. This is the Morning After's one-night-only stand-up special. Affectionate. Mostly true. Entirely their fault.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a 91-point lead, and he would like you to know that he achieved this through a combination of shrewd judgment and careful planning. He would like you to know that. What actually happened is he made a few decent signings, got fortunate with timing, and now spends the gap between fixtures trying to get AI tools to do what they claim they can do — a project that has consumed approximately four hundred hours of his life and produced, by his own admission, 'some interesting error messages.' The lead is real. The methodology is a work in progress. Tristan, in third, is watching the gap with the expression of a man who has done everything right and is still somehow behind the bloke who can't get ChatGPT to write a shopping list.

Speaking of doing everything right: Jeremy is fifth, 101 points off the lead, and is handling this with the serene composure of a man who knows, in his bones, that he is the cleverest person in the room. Jeremy built the spreadsheet this entire league runs on. He coded it from scratch. He could, if he wanted, build an algorithm to pick his team — and this is the important part — he simply doesn't want to. He picks on instinct, taste, and thirty years of watching football. He looks at Lloydy's AI experiment the way a master chef looks at a microwave. Technically a tool. Not really cooking.

Chris — Lloydy — is ninth, one point behind Tom, and still living with the consequences of a transfer window in which his algorithm fired off five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Five. In a row. The machine, apparently, had a very strong view on who should be playing in this tournament and that view was: people who were here last time. It was less a transfer engine and more a séance. It did eventually land one valid bid — a player who scored 8 points, which Lloydy mentions whenever possible — but the damage to his reputation among the other twelve managers is, let's say, ongoing. Jake, his main rival, has not stopped talking about it since. Jake, who is twelfth, who overspent catastrophically on Harry Kane, who injured himself on the way to the dentist — ON THE WAY, not at the dentist, not leaving the dentist, on the way there — Jake is vindicated, and he will not be letting this go. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' he shouts, to nobody in particular, before launching into a detailed conspiracy theory about what else Lloydy's algorithm might be running in his personal office complex. The dentist, for what it's worth, gave him a glowing report. He came home and told everyone.

Joe S is sixth, which is a respectable position for a man who spent the majority of his professional football career on a treatment table. Joe was, by all accounts, a genuinely brilliant player — flair, vision, the lot — and we will never fully know how good because he was injured for roughly half of it. He has carried this energy into fantasy football management: a man of evident quality, perpetually unavailable. He is also, famously, baffled by modern technology. He is currently in sixth place and is not entirely sure how the points system works. He is having a lovely time.

Now — the brothers. Sam is fourth, which is extraordinary for a man whose primary decision-making framework appears to be 'what would Shakespeare do.' Sam is a stage performer, a cricket devotee, a lover of beer and dancing, and a man who once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar — only to discover on moving day that the property had no bathroom. None. He spent a year in a house with a bar and no bathroom. He is currently fourth in a fantasy football league and this feels entirely consistent with his life. His brother Wigs is tenth, counselling everyone through their difficult moments with warmth and gentle wit, which is a beautiful quality in a human being and has done absolutely nothing for his points tally.

Nick is second. Nick is very tall, very loud, and on a football pitch an absolute menace — the kind of player who goes into a tackle not just late but with genuine creative ambition, leaving opponents wondering what exactly just happened to their ankles. His shots go approximately four feet over the crossbar with the trajectory of a man who learned to strike a ball by watching golf. He is second in the league. This is either inspiring or alarming depending on how close you are to him on a five-a-side pitch. His main rival is Tom, who is eighth, and who is — look, we're not going to say where Tom is right now, but there are reports, and they are incoherent, and whoever filed them sounded very happy.

Dave is eleventh, which gives him ample time to pursue his true ambition: a segment on The Culture Show called 'Trossy's Wafflecock,' in which he would hold forth on art, meaning, and the beautiful nothingness of human endeavour — ideally while smoking, eating something interesting, and playing the cymbals at a dramatically inappropriate moment. Dave loves a pun. Dave loves a city break. Dave is eleventh and he has written a poem about it that is, genuinely, better than this bulletin. Meanwhile Dan is seventh, living off-grid, being smart and witty and quietly excellent at this, allied as ever with Malik in thirteenth — Malik, managing from Iceland, who has built his squad almost entirely from players who have worn a Manchester United or Portugal shirt, and who has watched Portugal get eliminated with the stoic calm of a man who knew, on some level, that this was always how it was going to go.

The Final is coming. One match. One winner. Thirteen managers, one trophy, and a points gap that ranges from 'comfortable' to 'requiring a miracle.' Whatever happens next, it will be loud, it will be disputed, and Jake will almost certainly hurt himself getting to the venue. The Morning After will be there. It always is.
