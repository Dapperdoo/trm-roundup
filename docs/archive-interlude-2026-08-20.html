THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

No fixtures. No goals. No last-minute VAR heartbreak to process. The World Cup has reached the Final and decided to take a breath before the big one — which means, for one edition only, we turn the spotlight away from the pitch and point it squarely at the thirteen people who have been losing sleep over it. Pull up a chair. This is a roast, not a roundup. It's affectionate. Mostly.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and wearing the look of a man who has accidentally wandered into first place and is now desperately trying to appear like he planned it. He didn't dominate the transfer window — he made a few decent signings and the rest of the field obligingly fell over. Joe spends his actual free time trying to get AI tools to do what they promise. He has not yet succeeded. He is, in other words, losing two competitions simultaneously and winning one, which is a very specific kind of chaos. His main rival Tristan is breathing down his neck in third on 364, which means Joe's lead is about as comfortable as a camping chair at a three-day festival. Tristan, for his part, runs his entire operation off Jez's spreadsheet, manually checking every single number by hand on the FIFA website like a man photocopying the internet. He throws his hands up in disgust at least four times a day. We don't know at what, exactly, but we respect the commitment.

Sandwiched between them in second is Nick on 370, which is frankly alarming. Nick is very tall, very loud, and on a football pitch he is a reckless, clattering menace who flies into tackles like a man who has confused 'competitive sport' with 'personal vendetta'. His shooting, meanwhile, is best described as a golf swing — all backswing, enormous follow-through, ball disappearing somewhere over the stand and into a neighbouring postcode. The fact that this approach has him in second place tells you everything you need to know about fantasy football as a discipline. His main rival Tom is down in eighth on 321, which is the kind of gap that would sting — if Tom were currently in a position to feel it. Last reliable sighting of Tom placed him in a field somewhere, surrounded by people in tie-dye, nodding slowly to music that sounds like a dial-up modem having a spiritual experience. An eyewitness report, received this morning, read: 'yeah mate it's all… it's all very… the lasagne was incredible.' We're going to need more than that, Tom.

Fourth place belongs to Sam on 361, which is impressive for a man whose decision-making in non-football contexts is, to put it diplomatically, event-driven. Sam once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar. The house had no bathroom. He looked at the bar barrels and considered his options. He is a Shakespeare man, a cricket man, a theatre man — a man of genuine culture and wit, currently using those gifts to manage a fantasy football team one point behind Jeremy. Speaking of whom: Jeremy sits fifth on 360, quietly unimpressed by everything, picking his squad on pure instinct and taste the way a sommelier selects a wine — no algorithm, no system, just experience and a slightly withering expression. He built the spreadsheet this entire league runs on, from scratch, and has never once felt the need to mention it. He doesn't need to. He just looks at Lloydy and sighs.

Ah. Chris. Lloydy. Ninth place, 320 points, and the owner of the most chaotic transfer window in recent memory. His AI algorithm — lovingly assembled in what we can only imagine is a basement full of blinking servers and whiteboards — fired off five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Five. It held the entire league's transfer window hostage while it attempted to sign men who have since retired, aged out, or in some cases are probably coaching youth teams in the suburbs. It eventually landed one valid bid, which did at least score 8 points, so the machine is not entirely without merit. Jake has been vindicated. Jake has not stopped saying so. Jake — twelfth, 292 points, Snacob's Ladder — is a man who shouts 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' at random intervals to remind himself who he is, which suggests the grudges he's carrying may be taking up cognitive space he can't spare. He still hasn't forgiven Jeremy for the transfer correction from a couple of days ago, an act of administrative honesty that every other manager waved through without a second thought. Jake waved nothing through. Jake filed a grievance in his heart and laminated it. He also overspent badly on Harry Kane, which left the rest of his squad looking like a pub team assembled from a 'players available' bin. On the bright side, he's due a dentist appointment, which he is genuinely excited about — more excited, if we're honest, than his current league position warrants.

Down in sixth is Joe S on 338. Joe Sheerin: supremely gifted, universally beloved, the kind of footballer who, in full flow, made the game look like something invented specifically for him. He was also, for large portions of his career, horizontal. The treatment table saw more of Joe than the pitch did. Hamstrings, knees, ankles — if it could go wrong, it went wrong, often at the precise moment everyone had started believing again. He is also, famously, baffled by modern technology in a way that is both endearing and slightly alarming. We suspect he submitted his squad by writing the names on a piece of paper and handing it to someone younger. Seventh is Dan on 329 — off-grid, sharp as a tack, historically one of the best fantasy managers in this league, and allied with Malik in thirteenth on 280, who is managing from Iceland and has, at various points, signed what appears to be every player who has ever worn a Manchester United or Portugal shirt. Portugal, of course, have been eliminated. This has not visibly altered Malik's transfer philosophy. Dan and Malik operate as a kind of two-man think-tank: one living in the woods, one living in Reykjavik, both doing better than you'd expect and worse than they'd like.

Tenth place: Wigs, 319 points, Sam's brother, a counsellor by trade — which means he spends his professional life helping people process difficult emotions, and his leisure time watching his fantasy team narrowly underperform his sibling's. The sibling dynamic is doing a lot of heavy lifting in this league. Wigs is gregarious, gentle and funny, and he has the patience of a man who has heard everything. Eleventh is Dave on 300 — Trossy Ginge — lecturer, poet, lover of wordplay, player of cymbals on special occasions, and the man who still dreams of hosting a segment on The Culture Show called 'Trossy's Wafflecock', in which he would ramble warmly and at length about cultural nothingness and convince himself it meant something profound. Dave, we believe in the segment. We believe in you. We are less sure about the points tally, but poetry was never about the numbers.

So there we are. Thirteen managers. One table. One final still to come. Joe A leads, but only just. Jake is furious about at least three things simultaneously, one of them dental. Lloydy's algorithm is, presumably, currently trying to bid for Pelé. And somewhere in a field, Tom is nodding.

The World Cup Final is next. One match. Winner takes the trophy, and thirteen managers find out whether any of this was worth it. Rest up. The football is coming back — and it has no interest in being fair to anyone.
