THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. You're all getting it instead.

Good morning. No fixtures today. The tournament has paused for breath, the pitches are empty, the VAR officials are finally getting some sleep, and we find ourselves with nothing to report. No goals, no blanks, no heartbreak, no hauls. Just thirteen managers, sitting with their thoughts. Which, frankly, is more dangerous. So we're turning the microphone on the room. Pull up a chair. This is The Morning After: the interlude edition — a brief, affectionate, entirely unsolicited roast of every single person in this league. We go through all thirteen. Nobody leaves clean.

Let's start at the top, because that's where Joe A is, and he absolutely knows it. Four hundred and sixty-one points, first place, Shatner's Bassoon riding high. Tremendous. The only man in this league who has spent more time arguing with AI tools than actually watching football — and yet somehow he's winning. He opens a chatbot, asks it to do one simple thing, it hallucinates three paragraphs of confident nonsense, and he sits there, Guinness in hand, surrounded by empty cans and optimistic browser tabs, trying again. And again. It never works. He's documented every failure. He has a folder. He's basically running a one-man consumer-rights campaign against the entire AI industry, and his fantasy team is thriving despite it. Directly below him, Tristan — big man, sharp mind, genuinely does not suffer fools — is sitting at third on 364 points, glaring. Tristan does everything by hand. Every number, manually checked against the FIFA website. He prints nothing off Jez's spreadsheet without verifying it himself first. He is meticulous, he is correct, and he is furious that Joe A is still ninety-seven points ahead of him. You can practically hear him throwing his hands up from here.

Which brings us to Jeremy, fifth on 360 points, watching all of this with the quiet, measured composure of a man who built the spreadsheet everyone else is arguing over. The professor. Coded the whole infrastructure from scratch. Could, if he wanted, build an algorithm to pick his team in his sleep. Chooses not to. Picks purely on instinct, taste, experience — the organic approach. Looks at Lloydy's AI disaster and says nothing, because he doesn't need to. The expression says it all. Meanwhile, Chris — Lloydy, ninth on 320 — is somewhere in his personal office complex, surrounded by blinking servers and half-finished electrical projects, absolutely convinced the algorithm just needs one more tweak. The same algorithm that, in the last transfer window, fired five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Five. Before eventually landing one valid signing who, to be fair, scored eight points. One hit. Out of six attempts. This is the system. This is the lab. Jeremy has seen it all before and has simply chosen to feel nothing.

And then there's Jake. Jake, who is twelfth on 292 points, and who would like everyone to know — loudly, repeatedly, to anyone within earshot — that he told us so. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' he announces, to no one in particular, at least twice a day, just to keep his bearings. Jake despises the algorithm. He despises AI in general. He has theories about what Lloydy is doing with AI in his private life, in his office complex, possibly internationally, and none of the theories are reassuring. He cannot prove any of it. He doesn't need to. The 2022 bids are all the evidence he requires. What Jake can prove is that he injured himself on the way to the dentist. Not at the dentist. Not leaving the dentist. On the way there. He loves the dentist, by the way — genuinely, enthusiastically, in a way that concerns people — so the injury was especially cruel. He also spent a fortune on Harry Kane, filled the rest of the squad with players whose Wikipedia pages load in under two seconds, and still holds a grudge against Jeremy for a transfer correction made a couple of days ago that every other manager quietly accepted and moved on from. Every. Other. Manager. Jake is still on the phone about it.

Joe S is sixth on 338 points, which is a fine return from a man who spent roughly half his playing career on the treatment table. Back of the Van United — the name alone conjures a man who lived life at full tilt and paid for it in ligaments. Genuinely gifted footballer. Genuinely beloved. Also genuinely baffled by most technology made after 2003. If you send Joe S a voice note he will ring you back to ask what a voice note is. He is warm, funny, magnetic company — and he will absolutely do his hamstring getting out of the car to come and tell you so. Somewhere adjacent to this energy is Sam, fourth on 361 points, his expressive face doing about forty percent of his communication at any given moment. Stage performer, cricket enthusiast, Shakespeare devotee, lover of beer and dancing. His brother Wigs — tenth on 319, counsellor, gentle giant, fellow cricket man — is the steadier of the two O'Shea brothers, which is not a high bar. Sam once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar. The house had no bathroom. He discovered this on moving-in day. He stared at the empty bar barrels for a long time. Wigs, we assume, counselled him through it.

Over to Nick, second place, 370 points, Dyer's Rusty 9 Iron. The name is accurate. Nick on a football pitch is a six-foot-something reckless force of nature who flies into tackles like a man who has decided the laws of the game are more of a suggestion. Badly mistimed. Frequently deliberate. Opponents left blinking in the mud, wondering what just happened. Off the pitch: loud, warm, great company, loves his food and beer. On the pitch: a menace. His shooting is the golf swing — massive backswing, enormous follow-through, ball last seen clearing the stadium roof and landing in a neighbouring postcode. And yet: second. Somehow, second. His main rival Tom — eighth on 321 — is somewhere. We're not entirely sure where. Tom DJs, Tom travels, Tom is probably fine. He's always fine. He has never won this league despite years of genuine effort, which is the sort of thing that builds character. Tom is very characterful.

Dave is eleventh on 300 points, Trossy's Giants, and he would like to talk to you about culture. Specifically, he'd like to pitch you a TV segment — 'Trossy's Wafflecock', a proposed section of The Culture Show in which he rambles about cultural nothingness with tremendous sincerity and absolutely no conclusions. He plays the cymbals. Only on special occasions. He loves a pun, a city break, a cigarette, and a beer, and he will absolutely find a way to make all four feel like a profound statement about the human condition. Dan — seventh on 329, Denton Burn — lives off-grid, makes music, and is smart enough to find all of this deeply funny without saying so. His main ally is Malik, thirteenth on 280, managing Propaganda Parade from Iceland, which is a sentence that raises more questions than it answers. Malik's transfer strategy is simple: if they've worn a Manchester United shirt or a Portugal shirt, he's interested. Portugal, of course, are eliminated. Malik is taking this with Icelandic stoicism, which is to say: he is standing very still in a geothermal landscape, saying nothing, feeling everything.

Thirteen managers. One table. One tournament left to play. The Final is next — the last match, the last points, the last chance for anyone to make a move. Joe A leads, but ninety-seven points is not what it used to be when there's one game left. Tristan is sharpening his pencil. Nick is warming up his golf swing. Jake is already at the dentist, voluntarily, for fun. And somewhere, Lloydy's algorithm is preparing one final bid — probably for someone from 2018. See you on the other side.

The Final is the last fixture standing, and it is coming. Every point will matter. Every player will count. Thirteen managers, one last shot — and after that, we find out who actually deserved to be here. The Morning After will have its verdict ready. It always does.
