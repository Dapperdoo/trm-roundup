THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today — so we roast the managers instead.

There are no fixtures today. The World Cup pauses for breath, the pitches lie empty, and thirteen grown adults are left alone with their thoughts and their standings pages. Which means it's time to turn the spotlight away from the players — who at least get paid for this — and onto the managers themselves. Pull up a chair. This is going to be affectionate. Mostly.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a ninety-one-point lead, and he'd like you to know he's very relaxed about it. Genuinely. Extremely chill. He's spent the last three days trying to get an AI tool to summarise his squad depth for him, and it keeps hallucinating players who don't exist, generating confident nonsense in a very authoritative tone, and then crashing. Joe stares at the screen. He refreshes. He tries a different prompt. He is, by his own admission, fine. Meanwhile, Tristan — third, 364 points, glaring at that gap like a man who has just been told the sweetshop is closing — is doing it the old-fashioned way: hand-copying numbers from the FIFA website one by one, cross-referencing Jez's spreadsheet, throwing his hands up every twelve minutes. Two very different approaches. One of them has a ninety-one-point lead. Neither of them is sleeping.

Speaking of the spreadsheet: Jeremy, fifth on 360, built the entire results infrastructure this league runs on, from scratch, with his bare hands and a level of quiet competence that makes the rest of us feel slightly inadequate. He picks his team on instinct, taste and thirty years of watching football, and it works. He does not have an algorithm. He does not need one. He has simply been watching the game long enough to know things. He is, at this point, the league's equivalent of a master sommelier who doesn't need to read the label — while across town, Chris has a machine in his garage that is still, as of this morning, attempting to sign Lionel Messi's 2022 squad. Ninth place, 320 points, and the algorithm has at least stopped filing bids — for now. Jake, who predicted all of this and has the group-chat receipts to prove it, has not shut up about it since. He will not shut up about it. He is constitutionally incapable of shutting up about it.

Jake. Twelfth. 292 points. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' Right, yes, we know, Jake. We all know. Here is a man who spent a significant portion of his transfer budget on Harry Kane and then had to fill the rest of the squad with players whose Wikipedia pages are three sentences long. He also, in the past week, injured himself. Not at training. Not in a match. On the way to the dentist — which, in a twist that surprises no one who knows him, he was genuinely looking forward to. Jake loves the dentist the way most people love a long weekend. He arrived, eventually, limping. He also still hasn't forgiven Jeremy for the transfer correction that every other manager accepted in about four seconds flat. Every other manager said 'fair enough' and moved on. Jake is keeping a dossier. He has feelings about this. He would like to discuss them at length. He is also currently holding a tray of drinks on the dancefloor, and if you value your shirt, you will move.

The walking wounded, of course, are a proud tradition in this league, and Joe S — sixth, 338 points — is its founding patron. A man of genuine, luminous footballing talent who spent approximately half his playing career in a physio's waiting room, reading old magazines and being told to rest. The party lifestyle was always there to cushion the blow. Joe S approaches fantasy football the same way he approached a night out: with enormous enthusiasm, very little regard for what tomorrow holds, and a complete inability to explain to you how he got home. He is baffled by the technology involved in this league. He is delightful about it.

Over on the cultural wing, Dave — eleventh, 300 points — is composing a poem about his squad's recent performances. It has three stanzas, a volta, and a pun in the final couplet that he is very pleased with. Dave still believes, with the conviction of a man who has thought about it a great deal, that there is a slot on The Culture Show waiting for him — a segment called Trossy's Wafflecock, in which he would hold forth on the deeper meaning of things while the nation nodded along, slightly confused but oddly moved. He plays the cymbals. Only on special occasions. Nobody has ever asked him to stop, which is either a testament to his skill or to everyone's reluctance to have that conversation.

The brothers O'Shea: Sam, fourth on 361, and Wigs, tenth on 319. Sam — actor, Shakespeare devotee, cricket man, a person of enormous expressive range — once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar. Moved in. No bathroom. None. He eyed the empty barrels with the look of a man doing difficult calculations. Wigs, the counsellor of the two, is the sort of person who would have gently talked him through that moment, helped him process the feelings, and then also had a beer. They are both, in their own ways, exactly right.

Then there's Tom — eighth, 321 — who is managing his squad with one eye on the standings and the other on a horizon that probably involves a long flight and a field full of people. Nick, second on 370, is the league's most dangerous man in a five-a-side context: very tall, very loud, shoots like he's trying to clear a bunker on the back nine, and tackles like he's settling a personal score with someone's kneecap. That he is second in this league is either a vindication of his methods or a warning to the rest of us. Dan, seventh on 329, is living off-grid and doing better than most people who live on-grid, which feels like a comment on something. His ally Malik, propping up the table in thirteenth on 280, has had a rough tournament — Portugal are out, which means his beloved Seleção contingent have packed their bags and gone home, leaving him with a squad that now reads like a Manchester United injury list. He is managing from Iceland. He is doing his best. The heart wants what it wants, Malik, and apparently what it wants is players from Old Trafford.

The Final is coming. One match left. Thirteen managers, thirteen squads, and whatever happens next — someone is going to win this, someone is going to finish last, and Jake is going to injure himself celebrating either outcome. Stay tuned.

The Final awaits — one match, everything on the line, and The Morning After will be back with the full, definitive, points-have-been-counted reckoning. Don't go anywhere. Or do — just tell us what happened to your shoes.
