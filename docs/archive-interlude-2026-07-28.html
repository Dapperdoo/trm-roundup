THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

Good morning. There are no fixtures today — the tournament has paused for breath before the Final, which means the football has temporarily stopped being the problem and we have become the problem. So pull up a chair, because The Morning After is turning the spotlight on the thirteen people who have spent the last several weeks shouting at spreadsheets, refreshing FIFA pages, and making decisions of varying quality. This is the room. Let's work it.

We'll start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and someone needs to say something about it before his head gets any larger. Joe leads Shatner's Bassoon into the Final round with a 91-point cushion, which sounds comfortable until you remember that his main occupation between matchdays is trying to get AI tools to do what they claim they can do — a crusade that has, by all accounts, never once fully succeeded. Joe is essentially spending his free time arguing with chatbots while accidentally winning a fantasy football league. He didn't dominate the transfer window; he just made a few decent picks. Tristan, breathing down his neck in third on 364, would like everyone to know that. Tristan, who gleans every single data point by hand from the FIFA website — manually, heroically, one stat at a time — would very much like a word with Joe A about the gap. He has not had it yet. He will.

Nick is second on 370, which is genuinely impressive for a man whose entire footballing identity is built around launching shots approximately eleven metres over the crossbar as if he's attempting to clear a stand rather than score a goal. On the pitch Nick is also a reckless, clumping menace in the tackle — the kind of player who flies in two seconds late and then looks genuinely surprised at the carnage. Tall, loud, dangerous. His main rival is Tom, who is currently in eighth on 321 and — look, we're not going to say he's at a festival, we're just going to say that Tom's whereabouts at any given moment are best described as 'unclear, but apparently fine, and very happy about something.' The one eyewitness report we have from his direction this week was largely incoherent. He seemed to be enjoying himself enormously.

Let's talk about the walking wounded. Joe S — Back of the Van United, sixth on 338 — is a man who was, by all accounts, a genuinely brilliant footballer. Flair, vision, the lot. He also spent roughly half his career on a treatment table being told to rest a hamstring. The physio room knew his name better than his own teammates did. Joe S would turn up to training, look slightly too enthusiastic, and be injured by Thursday. He is universally loved, utterly baffled by modern technology, and we are delighted he is here. Then there is Jake. Jake — Snacob's Ladder, twelfth on 292 — has a similarly catastrophic relationship with his own body, except Jake injures himself doing nothing whatsoever. The standout incident, which this column will never stop mentioning, is that Jake once injured himself on his way to the dentist. On his way. To the dentist. A journey he was, by the way, extremely excited about, because Jake loves the dentist with a fervour that most people reserve for their wedding day. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' he will shout, apropos of nothing, possibly while nursing a new mystery injury sustained while putting his coat on.

Jake also has grievances. He has two, and they are load-bearing. The first: Jeremy — fifth on 360, the professor, the man who built the league's original spreadsheet from scratch and picks his team on pure instinct and taste, not code — recently corrected an honest transfer error and replaced an injured player Jake had bought with an uninjured one. Every other manager shrugged and moved on. Jake has not moved on. Jake is still there. The second: Chris. Lloydy — ninth on 320, the mad scientist, the man who built his own electrical kit and never sits still — ran his transfers through a self-constructed algorithm that, in its wisdom, fired off five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Four years out of date. It was essentially holding a séance. It finally landed one valid bid, and that player did at least score 8 points, which is the algorithmic equivalent of a stopped clock. Jake's paranoid conviction that Lloydy is secretly cooking up something even more unhinged in his private office complex has, if anything, been validated. Jeremy looked on in silence. Jeremy always looks on in silence. It is a very good silence.

Sam — fourth on 361, stage and TV performer, lover of beer and Shakespeare and cricket — is the kind of man who brings genuine wit and warmth to everything he does, which makes it all the more extraordinary that he once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar, only to discover on moving day that the property contained no bathroom. None. He eyed the empty bar barrels. He made calculations. We don't ask about the calculations. His brother Wigs — tenth on 319, counsellor, gentle giant, also a cricket man — is the more measured of the two O'Shea siblings, which is a low bar to clear but he clears it with dignity. Between them they have the emotional range of an entire theatre company and the cricket knowledge of a county scorer.

Dave — eleventh on 300, lecturer, poet, lover of puns and city breaks and cigarettes — is still pursuing his dream of a Culture Show segment called 'Trossy's Wafflecock', in which he would hold forth on cultural nothingness with the conviction of a man who has thought about it very hard and arrived somewhere interesting. He plays the cymbals, but only on special occasions. Nobody is entirely sure what qualifies as a special occasion. Dan — seventh on 329, musician, off-grid, historically one of the sharpest fantasy minds in this league — is allied with Malik, who is managing Propaganda Parade from Iceland in thirteenth on 280 and has signed, with great loyalty, anyone who has ever worn a Manchester United or Portugal shirt. Portugal, of course, have been eliminated. Malik is taking this with the quiet dignity of a man who knew exactly what he was doing and is not ready to discuss it.

And so here we are. Thirteen managers, one Final to go, and a league table that still has everything to play for — or at least, Joe A has everything to protect and twelve other people have everything to throw at him. Portugal are out. France are out. Argentina are out. The tournament has been ruthless. The managers have been, in their various ways, resilient, chaotic, sentimental, algorithmic, manual, injured, shouting their own team names, and in at least one case unsure which continent they are on. The Final is coming. The football will be back. Until then — this was the room, and we were very glad to be in it.

The Final is on the horizon, and with it the last points of the tournament. One match. Everything left on the table. The Morning After will be back when the whistle blows — and it will have numbers, and opinions, and very little mercy. See you on the other side.
