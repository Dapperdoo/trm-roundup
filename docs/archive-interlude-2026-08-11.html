THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football. Just thirteen people to mock.

There are no fixtures today. The tournament pauses, the pitches rest, and for once nobody is blanking, nobody is hauling, and nobody's goalkeeper is conceding a hat-trick of own goals in the 94th minute. Which means we have time — glorious, dangerous time — to turn the spotlight away from the football and shine it directly onto the people running these teams. Ladies and gentlemen, pull up a chair. We're doing the managers.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a fairly comfortable lead, and he would like you to know that this is the result of careful, considered management. What it is actually the result of is making a few decent signings before anyone else got organised, and then spending the intervening weeks locked in furious combat with AI tools that keep promising to summarise his scouting notes and then producing four paragraphs about a completely different sport. Joe A's entire fantasy operation is a man at a laptop, swearing quietly, clicking 'regenerate' for the forty-seventh time. He's winning the league. He cannot get a chatbot to do a single useful thing. These facts coexist without irony.

Hot on his heels — and I mean hot, because the gap is genuinely closing — are Nick, Tristan, Sam and Jeremy, all within a hundred points of the summit and all absolutely convinced they're the rightful leader. Nick is second, which is remarkable for a man whose entire footballing philosophy is to hit the ball as hard as possible in roughly an upward direction and see what God decides. On the pitch he's a six-foot-something wrecking ball who goes into tackles like he's trying to separate someone's soul from their body — frequently on purpose — and yet here he is, second place, composed and dangerous. His main rival is Tom, who is currently somewhere between a field and a flight path and cannot be reached for comment. Tristan is third, running his operation off Jez's spreadsheet, entering every number by hand from the FIFA website with the focused intensity of a man who does not trust machines and will not be told otherwise. He throws his hands up in disgust approximately four times per matchday. He is also, quietly, very good at this.

Sam is fourth, which his brother Wigs — tenth — is pretending not to notice. Sam is a man of the theatre, a lover of Shakespeare, beer, dancing, and at least one house that had a bar but absolutely no bathroom. He once fell over so completely on a night out that both shoes departed his feet simultaneously, as if they'd made a joint decision to leave. He is witty, scatty, and probably thinking about cricket. Wigs, meanwhile, is a counsellor, which means he spends his professional life helping people process difficult emotions — and his fantasy football life experiencing them. He is warm, gentle, and quietly seething at being thirty-one points behind his own brother. That's fine. That's healthy. He would tell you so himself.

Jeremy is fifth on 360 points, which is exactly where you'd expect a man of his calibre to be: close enough to threaten, measured enough not to panic. Jeremy built the spreadsheet that runs this entire league. He is, by any reasonable measure, the most technically equipped person in this competition. He picks his team on instinct, taste and experience alone — no algorithm, no system, just the accumulated wisdom of someone who has been doing this long enough to know what he's looking at. He watches Chris's algorithm saga with the quiet, withering expression of a professor watching a student try to prove two plus two equals five using a very confident PowerPoint. Chris, for his part, is ninth on 320 points, which is what happens when your AI tries to sign four players from the 2022 World Cup before anyone can stop it. The one valid bid it eventually landed did score 8 points. The algorithm would like a round of applause. Jeremy would like a word.

Which brings us to Jake. Jake is twelfth on 292 points, and Jake is furious — not just about the standings, not just about Harry Kane eating roughly forty percent of his entire transfer budget and then performing with the serene indifference of a man on a very expensive holiday — but about Jeremy. Specifically, about the transfer correction from a couple of days ago, in which Jeremy swapped out an injured player Jake had bought and replaced him with a healthy alternative. Every other manager shrugged and moved on. Jake has not moved on. Jake will not move on. Jake has filed the incident away in a mental cabinet labelled 'UNRESOLVED' and visits it regularly. He is also, separately, convinced that Chris is doing something sinister with AI across his entire office complex — some vast, humming, algorithmic conspiracy that Jake alone can see. In between these grievances he shouts 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' at intervals throughout the day, just to centre himself. He injured himself last week. He was on his way to the dentist. He loves the dentist. The dentist, presumably, loves him back.

Joe S is sixth on 338 points, which is a solid return for a man who spent the better part of his football career horizontal. Joe S was a genuinely brilliant player — genuinely, no exaggeration — and he was injured for roughly half of it. The physio room knew his name before his teammates did. He also, it must be said, approached the off-pitch side of professional football with considerable enthusiasm. He is now utterly baffled by modern technology, which means he and Joe A have something in common: both are losing battles with machines, just from opposite ends.

Down in seventh, Dan is living off-grid and quietly brilliant, as he always is. His ally Malik is thirteenth — last — managing from Iceland, signing anyone who has ever pulled on a Manchester United or Portugal shirt, and watching both of those nations get knocked out of the tournament. Portugal are gone. Eliminated. Which means Malik's entire scouting philosophy has been invalidated by the bracket. Dan, to his credit, has said nothing. That is what allies are for. And then there is Dave — eleventh, 300 points, a poet, a lecturer, a man who plays the cymbals on special occasions and still dreams of hosting a segment on The Culture Show in which he would ramble, beautifully and at length, about absolutely nothing in particular. He loves wordplay. He loves puns. He loves a city break and a cigarette. He is, in the truest sense, a man who contains multitudes. The multitudes are currently eleventh.

The World Cup Final is next. One match. One winner. Thirteen managers, all of them convinced — on some level, in the quiet of their own heads — that they've got this. Jake will shout his team name at the wall. Joe A will ask an AI to help him and be told something unhelpful. Jeremy will look at the numbers, trust his gut, and say nothing. And somewhere out there, Tom is probably fine. Probably. Fixtures return soon — and when they do, so does the football, the points, the chaos, and the blessed relief of having something to write about other than all of you.

The World Cup Final is on the horizon. One game to end it all — and with it, the final reckoning between thirteen managers who have spent weeks scheming, refreshing, and occasionally injuring themselves en route to the dentist. Rest up. The last points of the tournament are coming, and this time, there is nowhere left to hide.
