THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

There are no fixtures today. None. Zip. The World Cup has reached the Final and is holding its breath, and so — apparently — are we. Which means for one special edition, we put the football down entirely and turn the spotlight onto the thirteen people who have been absolutely torturing themselves over it for weeks. Ladies and gentlemen, your managers.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting on 461 points and a nine-year-old laptop that is perpetually running seventeen browser tabs of AI tools that have all, individually, promised to change his life and delivered approximately nothing. Shatner's Bassoon leads this league by a distance that would be comfortable if Tristan weren't breathing down his neck from third like a large, sweet-eating Russian man who has personally read every line of the FIFA website by hand. Tristan doesn't use algorithms. Tristan doesn't use shortcuts. Tristan just sits there, squinting at the data, eating a Haribo, and throwing his hands up at the screen. Between Joe A's broken AI subscriptions and Tristan's artisanal number-crunching, it's basically a battle between the future and a man who prefers to do it properly.

Then there's Nick in second. One hundred points behind the leader, which is fine, except Nick's real problem has never been the fantasy league — it's that on an actual football pitch he is a six-foot-something heat-seeking missile in boots who launches himself at opponents like a man who has confused a five-a-side pitch with a demolition derby. The shooting is worse. Nick hits the ball with the energy of someone attempting a rescue shot from the rough on the 18th hole, and it goes roughly where golf balls go: miles over, slightly right, into someone's garden. His main rival is Tom, who is almost certainly not worried, because Tom is almost certainly somewhere that doesn't have a signal.

Sam is fourth, which is impressive for a man whose primary skill set is Shakespeare, cricket, and finding the only house in the postcode that has a bar but no bathroom. His brother Wigs is tenth — counsellor, cricket lover, genuinely one of the warmest people in the room — and together the O'Shea brothers bracket this league like a pair of very affable bookends. Sam will have something witty to say about the gap between them. Wigs will probably let him finish.

Dave is eleventh, which is a shame, because Dave is the kind of man who should be winning things. He's a lecturer, a poet, a lover of wordplay, a city-break connoisseur, and he plays the cymbals — but only on special occasions, which is exactly the right policy for cymbals. He has long dreamed of a Culture Show segment called Trossy's Wafflecock, in which he would hold forth on cultural nothingness with tremendous sincerity. Honestly? Given the state of arts television, he'd get commissioned. Eleventh place, though. The cymbals remain in the case.

Jeremy is fifth, which is quietly, classically Jeremy. The man built the spreadsheet that runs this entire league from scratch — the very spreadsheet Tristan is currently hunched over — and he picks his team on instinct, taste and hard-won experience, not code. He could build an algorithm. He simply chooses not to. He watches Chris's AI misadventures with the expression of a professor watching a student try to microwave a dissertation. Measured. Swiss. Faintly, magnificently unimpressed.

Speaking of which. Chris. Ninth. Lloydy's algorithm — his pride, his baby, his contribution to the history of football analytics — recently held up the entire league's transfer window by submitting five consecutive bids for players from the 2022 World Cup. Not 2026. 2022. Players who have, in several cases, since retired, aged out, or simply moved on with their lives. The bot was essentially holding a séance. It eventually landed one valid bid, the player scored 8 points, and Chris has been dining out on that 8 ever since. Jake, who has been warning us about this for months, has never looked more vindicated, and he would like you to know that. He would very much like you to know that. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' he will shout, to no one in particular, before limping slightly from an injury sustained last Tuesday on his way to the dentist — which he loved, by the way. Brilliant appointment. Highly recommended. Jake is twelfth, still furious at Jeremy over the transfer correction that every other manager quietly accepted, still convinced that Lloydy is doing something sinister with AI in his office complex, and still carrying a tray of drinks onto a dancefloor that will not end well for anyone nearby.

Dan is seventh, off-grid and unbothered, which is exactly the correct response to being seventh. His ally Malik is thirteenth — managing from Iceland, signing anyone who has ever pulled on a Manchester United or Portugal shirt, which is a romantic transfer policy that becomes complicated when you notice Portugal have just been eliminated. Malik's scouting network is loyal, globe-spanning, and approximately one country short. Joe S is sixth: Back of the Van United, built by a man whose playing career was genuinely brilliant in the stretches between the physio appointments, the treatment tables, and the various structural failures of a body that simply refused to cooperate. He is universally beloved, baffled by his own phone, and would absolutely still nutmeg you.

The Final is coming. Thirteen managers, one trophy, and a league table that is about to be settled by ninety minutes of football none of us can control. Get some rest. Jake, please take a cab to the dentist. Chris, unplug the algorithm. Everyone else: brace yourselves.

The World Cup Final is the next — and last — fixture on the calendar. One match. Everything on the line. The Morning After will be back to cover it, and given what's at stake for every manager in this table, it promises to be the most chaotic, joyful, agonising edition of the season. Don't be late.
