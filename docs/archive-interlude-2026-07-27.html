THE MORNING AFTER

Interlude Special
No football today. The managers are the entertainment.

Right. There are no fixtures today. None. The World Cup has paused, the pitches are empty, the VAR officials are briefly at peace, and we find ourselves with nothing to report — which means we turn the spotlight away from the players and shine it, mercilessly, on the thirteen people who picked them. Pull up a chair. This is the room.

Let's start at the top, because Joe A is sitting there in first place on 461 points and he deserves exactly thirty seconds of credit before we move on. Thirty seconds. Done. Because here is what Joe A actually does with his free time: he argues with AI. Not recreationally — obsessively. He will spend four hours trying to get a language model to produce a single coherent paragraph, screenshot the failure, paste it into a different tool, watch that one hallucinate, and then write a very measured thread about it that nobody reads. He is, in other words, doing the same thing as Lloydy's algorithm, just with more self-awareness and less damage to the transfer window. His lead is real. His methods are chaotic. Tristan is coming.

Speaking of Tristan — third, 364 points, breathing hard — he runs his entire operation off a spreadsheet that Jeremy built and gleans every single number by hand from the FIFA website. By hand. In 2026. The man is essentially a Victorian clerk, hunched over a ledger, transcribing figures with a quill, except the quill is a trackpad and the ledger is a Google Sheet and at any moment he might throw his hands up in disgust at what he finds there. And yet: third place. Turns out the artisanal approach works. Meanwhile Jeremy, who actually built the spreadsheet, is sitting fifth on 360 points, which is the intellectual equivalent of inventing the printing press and then losing a spelling bee. Jeremy picks his team on instinct, taste and experience — no algorithm, no system, just the accumulated wisdom of a brilliant football brain — and he is quietly, devastatingly unimpressed by anyone who does it differently. He says nothing. He doesn't have to.

Now. Jake. Snacob's Ladder. He will shout that at you unprompted. 'SNACOB'S LADDER!' — usually at a volume that suggests he's forgotten you're standing right next to him. Jake is last but one on 292 points, which is a position he has achieved despite, or possibly because of, spending a significant portion of his transfer budget on Harry Kane and the remainder on players whose names he learned phonetically. He is also, it must be said, a man who injures himself at a rate that would concern a medical professional. He has hurt himself doing nothing. He once injured himself on the way to the dentist — which, for Jake, is the cruelest possible irony, because he LOVES the dentist. Adores it. Will talk about his dental hygienist with genuine warmth and enthusiasm. The dentist is the one appointment he never misses, and his body chose that exact journey to betray him. He is currently furious at Jeremy over the transfer correction from a couple of days ago — an honest administrative fix that every other manager waved through without a second thought — and Jake alone is still carrying it around like a stone in his shoe. On the dancefloor tonight he will absolutely be holding a tray of drinks. Someone will get wet. It is not a question of if.

Jake's rival Chris — Lloydy, ninth, 320 points — is the league's mad scientist, a man who bikes, travels, builds his own electrical kit and, most recently, unleashed an AI algorithm on the transfer window that promptly had a small breakdown and attempted to sign five players from the 2022 World Cup. Four years out of date. It wasn't building a squad; it was holding a séance. The algorithm did eventually land one valid bid — a player who scored 8 points, which Lloydy will tell you about — but the damage to the window, and to his reputation, was done. Jake's paranoid fantasies about what Lloydy is secretly doing with AI in his personal office complex are, at this point, probably the most reasonable thing Jake believes.

Fourth place belongs to Sam, and Sam deserves a moment because Sam is genuinely one of the wittiest people in this league and still somehow manages to make decisions that defy all wit. This is a man who once signed a year-long lease on a house because the viewing had its own bar. Day one of moving in: no bathroom. None. He stood in a property he was legally committed to for twelve months, surrounded by empty barrels, doing the maths. He is also a man who, after one long night, fell over so completely that both his shoes came off simultaneously — not one shoe, both — which suggests a level of commitment to the fall that most people simply cannot achieve. He loves Shakespeare, cricket, beer and dancing, and he runs a fantasy team called 'Look at his face. Just Look at his FACE!' which is, honestly, the correct energy for all of this.

Sam's brother Wigs is tenth on 319 points, which puts the two brothers within 42 points of each other and raises the question of whether their Sunday phone calls are still warm and fraternal or have quietly become scouting reports delivered in code. Wigs is a counsellor by trade — gentle, gregarious, genuinely good at listening — which makes him uniquely qualified to process the emotional experience of a mid-table fantasy season with grace and equanimity. The rest of us are just suffering. Nearby in the table, Dave is eleventh on 300 points and dealing with it in the way Dave deals with everything: through wordplay, a cigarette, and the lingering dream of presenting a segment on The Culture Show called 'Trossy's Wafflecock', in which he would hold forth on cultural nothingness with the conviction of a man who has genuinely convinced himself it means something. Dave, if it helps: eleventh place is a rich seam of material.

Down in thirteenth — last, 280 points — is Malik, managing from Iceland with a transfer policy so pure it borders on philosophical: if you have worn a Manchester United or Portugal shirt, you are in. Portugal are eliminated. This has not been a great tournament for the philosophy. His ally Dan is seventh on 329 points, living off-grid and somehow still more plugged into this league than people with functioning broadband. Dan is historically one of the best fantasy managers in the room, which makes his current mid-table position feel like a deliberate artistic choice — the musical equivalent of playing quietly when you could fill the room. He'll turn it up. Meanwhile Nick is second on 370 points, which is extraordinary for a man whose entire footballing identity is built around hammering shots six feet over the crossbar and launching into tackles that opponents will remember for the wrong reasons. Nick plays football like he's trying to make a point about something, and nobody is entirely sure what the point is, but it is LOUD.

Which leaves us with Joe S — sixth, 338 points — a man who was, by all accounts, a genuinely gifted footballer, the kind of player who could do things on a pitch that made people stop and watch. He spent roughly half his career on a treatment table. Injuries found Joe S the way rain finds a picnic. He is universally loved, utterly baffled by any technology invented after 2003, and still somehow more coordinated than Jake. And then there's Tom, eighth on 321 points, whose whereabouts at any given moment are genuinely unclear. He is somewhere. He is probably fine. He is almost certainly happy about it.

That's your lot. No fixtures to preview because — as the data will tell you — the next match date is currently unknown, which means the tournament is either building to something enormous or the scheduling committee is also having a lull. Either way, the managers will be here, refreshing their phones, second-guessing their squads, and in at least one case shouting their own team name into the middle distance. We'll be back when the football is.
